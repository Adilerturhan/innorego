## Question 1

The emergence of generative AI platforms (e.g., large language models, AI-generated imagery, or synthetic media) presents both opportunities for innovation and significant regulatory challenges. Choose one specific real-world application of generative AI—such as AI-assisted legal writing tools, deepfake video creation platforms, or AI-generated medical advice systems—and develop a comprehensive regulatory approach for it.

In your response, you must:

1.  Select and briefly describe the chosen AI application, including how it relies on data-driven innovation. (2 points)
2.  Identify and apply at least three different regulatory strategies (e.g., command-and-control, incentive-based, market-harnessing, rights and liabilities, disclosure, design-based, or risk-based approaches) as discussed in Baldwin, Cave & Lodge's framework. (9 points)
3.  Critically analyze the strengths and weaknesses of each strategy in the context of your chosen AI application. (9 points)
4.  Anticipate and discuss potential regulatory disconnects that may arise (e.g., due to the pacing problem, ambiguity, or over/under-regulation). (5 points)

**Answer:**

**1. Chosen AI application:**

I focus on AI-assisted legal writing tools (e.g., tools like Harvey or DraftWise), which use large language models (LLMs) to generate contracts, legal opinions, or case summaries. These platforms rely heavily on training data composed of legal texts, case law, and other documents to simulate human legal reasoning and drafting.

**2. Application of regulatory strategies:**

**A. Rights and liabilities:**
Allocating clear liability for harm caused by incorrect or biased AI-generated legal content is essential. For example, if a tool suggests invalid or misleading clauses, clients could suffer financial or legal harm. Under this strategy, liability could rest with either the software provider or the legal firm using the tool.

**B. Risk-based regulation:**
Legal AI tools could be classified as “high-risk” given their societal impact, particularly in sensitive cases (e.g., criminal law, immigration). A regulatory body could impose stricter risk assessments before deployment, like mandatory audits or bias testing.

**C. Disclosure:**
Developers could be required to disclose how their AI systems were trained, how outputs are generated, and what limitations exist. Legal firms could also be obligated to inform clients when AI assistance has been used in their documentation.

**3. Strengths and weaknesses of the strategies**

**A. Rights and liabilities**
*   **Strengths:** Incentivizes providers and users to ensure responsible use and testing.
*   **Weaknesses:** Legal causality is hard to establish in AI systems. High enforcement costs and uncertainty may reduce innovation incentives.

**B. Risk-based**
*   **Strengths:** Aligns regulation with actual harm potential. Increases responsiveness to evolving risks.
*   **Weaknesses:** As noted in the lecture, risk scoring is political, and some tools might “fly under the radar". The high data burden may limit smaller innovators.

**C. Disclosure**
*   **Strengths:** Enhances transparency and trust. Informed consumers are more capable of assessing risks.
*   **Weaknesses:** Information asymmetries persist, and disclosures might be too technical to be useful. It's also costly for companies to produce and maintain accessible documentation.

**4. Regulatory disconnect:**

AI-generated legal tools exemplify the “pacing problem” identified by Brownsword: legal tech is moving faster than the legal frameworks regulating professional responsibility. There is ambiguity in applying existing legal standards (e.g., unauthorized practice of law), and Section 230-like shields may not apply in all jurisdictions.

**Risk:** Overregulation could stifle innovation in small firms, while underregulation risks undermining the legal profession and client safety.
