### Model answer

**Introduction and Traditional Framework:**

Big Data refers to the massive volume of data generated daily—Forbes reported that we generate almost 2.5 quintillion bytes of data every day. Data is now considered "the new oil" and "the new currency of the digital economy" (`Module_3/Lecture Competition Law - MOK - FINAL.md:82-84`). EU competition law traditionally pursues several interconnected objectives: facilitation and safeguarding of the internal market, protection of consumer welfare, promotion of economic efficiency (allocative, cost, and dynamic), and protection of the competitive process itself (`Module_3/Generated/consolidated_module_3_summary.md:69-92`). These objectives are implemented through three main pillars: Article 101 TFEU (prohibition of anti-competitive agreements and concerted practices), Article 102 TFEU (prohibition of abuse of dominant position), and merger control (`Lecture:139-143`). Competition is traditionally assessed across five parameters: price, quality, output, choice, and innovation (`Lecture:114`).

**Challenges to Traditional Parameters:**

Big Data challenges each of the five traditional competition parameters in fundamental ways. First, **price analysis** becomes inadequate when services are "free" and advertisement-dependent, yet data is the main currency—how can competition authorities measure harm when the monetary price equals zero? (`Lecture:116`). Second, **quality concerns** now must incorporate privacy and data protection issues. The German Meta/Bundeskartellamt case established that data protection violations can be taken into account as competition concerns when assessing abuse of dominance, with the CJEU confirming that data protection considerations can be integrated into competition law analysis (`Lecture:446-452`). Third, regarding **output**, data's non-rivalrous nature complicates monopolization analysis. Unlike tangible goods, "data ownership" does not necessarily imply market foreclosure because multiple companies can use the same data in parallel (`Data and competition law (Podszun & Kreifels).md:79-89`). Fourth, **choice** is constrained by network effects and data accumulation creating barriers to entry, reducing the availability of alternative providers (`Lecture:119`). Fifth, **innovation** presents a tension: competition law must simultaneously facilitate data-driven innovation while preventing data concentration from hindering it, raising the question of whether competition law helps or hinders innovation in data-driven markets (`Lecture:120`).

**Challenges to Traditional Tools:**

The three competition law tools face implementation challenges in data-driven markets. Under **Article 101 TFEU**, information sharing among competitors becomes problematic—sharing sensitive business data such as algorithms, pricing models, or user engagement strategies may constitute prohibited concerted practices or cartels. Competitors may face fines for sharing such information without running afoul of the coordination prohibition (`Podszun & Kreifels:95`). Under **Article 102 TFEU**, the Microsoft case established precedent that refusing to disclose essential interface data can constitute abuse of dominant position (`Lecture:432-444`). However, applying the essential facilities doctrine to Big Data raises questions about what constitutes "indispensable" data in data-driven markets. **Merger control** must now assess data concentration concerns, as evidenced by several major technology mergers: Google/DoubleClick (2008) raised concerns about combining users' search behavior with web-browsing behavior; Facebook/WhatsApp (2014) involved concerns about data concentration for targeted advertising; Microsoft/LinkedIn (2016) presented similar data concentration issues; and Google/Fitbit (2020) resulted in imposition of a data silo to address data concentration concerns (`Lecture:300-308`).

**Conclusion:**

Big Data challenges traditional competition law methods by making competitive harm harder to detect in zero-price markets, requiring the integration of data protection considerations into competition analysis, and demanding new analytical frameworks for non-rivalrous assets. Large online service providers are gaining overwhelming advantage by accumulating Big Data, yet traditional competition tools may struggle to address these issues effectively. The fundamental question posed by the course materials remains: Are traditional competition tools "fit for purpose" for data-driven markets? (`Lecture:122`; `Podszun & Kreifels:75-94`).

## Question 2 — Case Study: ShopLink and Article 102 TFEU (6 pts)

**Conclusion (required):** Yes, ShopLink's conduct likely constitutes an abuse of dominant position under Article 102 TFEU.

### Model answer

**Legal Issue:** Whether ShopLink's algorithmic self-preferencing constitutes an abuse of dominant position under Article 102 TFEU.

**Rule — Article 102 TFEU Test:**

Article 102 TFEU prohibits "any abuse by one or more undertakings of a dominant position within the internal market or in a substantial part of it" insofar as it may affect trade between Member States (`Lecture:395`). The legal test requires establishing five elements: (1) undertaking, (2) dominant position, (3) abuse, (4) in substantial part of internal market, and (5) no objective justification (`Lecture:404-411`; `ACD Competition Law 2025.md:25-28`). Article 102(a)-(d) provides non-exhaustive examples of abuse, including "limiting production, markets or technical development to the prejudice of consumers" and "applying dissimilar conditions to equivalent transactions with other trading parties, thereby placing them at a competitive disadvantage" (`Lecture:397-402`).

**Application:**

**1) Undertaking**

*Analysis:* An undertaking is "any entity that engages in commercial/economic activities" whether or not such activities are carried out for profit (`Lecture:420`; `ACD:32`). ShopLink operates an online marketplace connecting independent sellers with buyers, which constitutes commercial/economic activity.

*Conclusion:* Met. ShopLink is an undertaking.

**2) Dominant Position**

*Analysis:* A dominant position exists when an undertaking possesses "a position of economic strength which enables the undertaking to prevent effective competition by giving it the power to behave to an appreciable extent independently of its competitors and consumers" (`Lecture:422`).

For dominance assessment, three factors are relevant:

(a) **Market definition:** The relevant product market is online marketplace platform services for connecting sellers and buyers. The geographic market is the EU or a substantial part of it.

(b) **Market share and market position:** ShopLink is described as a "leading online marketplace in the EU." While the exact market share is not specified, "leading" suggests significant market power. Assuming "leading" means market share exceeding 40% by analogy to case law precedent, this would indicate a potentially dominant position.

(c) **Barriers to entry:** Network effects are inherent in two-sided platforms—the more sellers use ShopLink, the more buyers are attracted, and vice versa. Additionally, ShopLink possesses accumulated data advantages about buyer preferences and seller performance. These factors create significant barriers to entry for competing marketplaces.

(d) **Vertical integration:** ShopLink operates both the marketplace platform and sells its own "ShopLink Basics" branded products, creating a conflict of interest where it acts as both platform operator and competing seller.

*Conclusion:* Likely dominant. Stating assumption: "Assuming 'leading' means market share exceeding 40% and significant barriers to entry created by network effects and data advantages, ShopLink likely holds a dominant position."

**3) Abuse**

*Analysis:* "There is no single definition for an abuse" but Article 102(a)-(d) provides non-exhaustive examples (`Lecture:418`; `ACD:34`). Crucially, "a dominant position in itself is not an infringement of Article 102 TFEU – only the abuse of its dominance to reduce/distort competition in the market. This gives a special responsibility to a dominant company" (`Lecture:428`).

*Type of abuse:* **Exclusionary abuse** (not exploitative). The conduct excludes competitors from effective market access rather than directly exploiting consumers through unfair pricing.

*Conduct analysis:* ShopLink changed its search algorithm so that its own products appear at the top of search results "regardless of relevance or consumer ratings." This algorithmic self-preferencing constitutes abuse under:

- **Article 102(c):** "applying dissimilar conditions to equivalent transactions with other trading parties, thereby placing them at a competitive disadvantage" (`Lecture:401`). ShopLink treats its own products differently from independent sellers' products despite both being sold through the same marketplace.

- **Article 102(b):** "limiting markets or technical development to the prejudice of consumers" (`Lecture:400`). By prioritizing its own products regardless of relevance, ShopLink limits the effective functioning of the marketplace and prejudices consumers who receive less relevant search results.

*Effects:*

- **Harm to competitors:** Competing sellers suffer reduced visibility and sales (stated explicitly in the facts). The algorithmic manipulation forecloses competitors from effective market access.

- **Harm to consumers:** Consumers receive reduced choice quality because less relevant results are prioritized over more relevant ones. Innovation incentives are reduced because sellers have less incentive to improve product quality or ratings if the algorithm does not reward such improvements.

*Parallel to precedent:* The Microsoft case established that exclusionary conduct foreclosing competitors from market access constitutes abuse (`Lecture:432-444`; `ACD:34-35`). While Microsoft withheld essential interface information, ShopLink manipulates algorithmic visibility to achieve similar exclusionary effects—preventing competitors from effectively reaching customers despite being present on the platform.

*Special responsibility:* Dominant companies have a "special responsibility" not to impair genuine competition (`Lecture:428`). ShopLink's algorithmic manipulation directly impairs the competitive process by substituting algorithmic favoritism for merit-based competition.

*Conclusion:* Met. Self-preferencing "regardless of relevance or consumer ratings" constitutes exclusionary abuse under Article 102(b) and (c).

**4) Substantial Part of Internal Market**

*Analysis:* "Dominance in already one EU Member State is sufficient" to satisfy this element (`Lecture:424`; `ACD:28`). ShopLink operates as a "leading online marketplace in the EU," indicating EU-wide or at least multi-Member State operations.

*Conclusion:* Met. ShopLink's EU-wide marketplace operations affect a substantial part of the internal market.

**5) No Objective Justification**

*Analysis:* Even if the first four elements are met, conduct may be justified if objectively necessary and proportionate (`Lecture:426`).

*Possible justifications ShopLink might argue:*

1. **Quality control:** "ShopLink Basics" products meet certain quality standards; prioritizing them protects consumers from low-quality products.

2. **Efficiency gains:** Vertical integration reduces transaction costs; algorithm optimization for own products creates efficiencies that benefit the overall marketplace.

3. **Consumer preference:** Consumers prefer ShopLink-branded products, and the algorithm merely reflects this preference.

*Counter-arguments:*

1. **Quality control is achievable without algorithmic manipulation:** Independent consumer ratings already exist and provide quality signals. ShopLink's algorithm actively ignores these ratings ("regardless of... consumer ratings"), indicating quality is not the true motivation. Quality can be ensured through minimum standards or labeling without foreclosing competitors.

2. **Efficiency gains must benefit consumers:** By analogy to Article 101(3) TFEU standards, efficiency justifications require that gains are passed on to consumers (`ACD:36`). Here, the conduct reduces choice quality for consumers by deprioritizing more relevant products. The efficiency gains, if any, accrue only to ShopLink, not to consumers or the market.

3. **Consumer preference should be tested in competitive markets:** If consumers truly prefer ShopLink-branded products, this preference would emerge organically through competitive marketplace dynamics. Algorithmic imposition of preference prevents consumers from exercising genuine choice.

*Legal standard for justification:* Justification must be **objective** (based on legitimate business reasons, not merely self-interest) and **proportionate** (necessary and not exceeding what is required to achieve the legitimate objective) (`Lecture:426`).

*Application of proportionality:* The phrase "regardless of relevance or consumer ratings" is critical. The conduct is not tailored to any legitimate objective—it prioritizes ShopLink products even when they are less relevant or lower-rated than competitors' products. This indicates the conduct is disproportionate and exceeds what would be necessary for any legitimate purpose such as quality control or efficiency.

*Conclusion:* Likely not objectively justified. The "regardless of relevance" nature of the algorithmic manipulation indicates the conduct is disproportionate and serves primarily to exclude competitors rather than to achieve any legitimate business objective.

**Enforcement Challenges:**

Competition authorities face several practical enforcement challenges when addressing algorithmic self-preferencing cases like ShopLink:

1. **Algorithmic opacity and technical complexity:** Search algorithms are proprietary trade secrets with complex weighting factors. Authorities need specialized technical expertise to detect self-preferencing, reverse-engineer algorithmic biases, and prove the causal link between algorithm changes and competitive harm. Demonstrating that the algorithm change caused the reduction in competitors' visibility and sales requires sophisticated econometric analysis (`inferred from Podszun & Kreifels:48`).

2. **Market definition in two-sided platforms:** Digital marketplaces operate as two-sided platforms connecting sellers and buyers. This creates market definition challenges: Is the relevant market the seller-side (platform services for independent sellers), the buyer-side (marketplace services for consumers), or both? Additionally, consumers often access marketplaces "for free," complicating traditional harm analysis that focuses on price effects (`Lecture:114-122`). Defining the relevant market boundaries—all online marketplaces, product-specific marketplaces, or other alternatives—becomes complex.

3. **Evidence gathering and business confidentiality:** Authorities need access to proprietary algorithm code, algorithm change logs, performance data showing how the algorithm ranks products, and competing sellers' sales data to quantify harm. ShopLink may claim business confidentiality protections over algorithm code and sensitive commercial data. Balancing the need for evidence with legitimate confidentiality concerns presents practical difficulties (`inferred from Podszun & Kreifels:48`).

4. **Speed of enforcement versus innovation pace:** Algorithms change frequently—ShopLink could modify its algorithm multiple times during a multi-year investigation. Enforcement procedures (investigation, statement of objections, decision, appeals) may take years. By the time a final decision is reached, the algorithm may have changed, potentially rendering the decision obsolete. Interim measures to prevent ongoing harm are legally available but complex to design and implement (`inferred from pacing problem discussion`).

5. **Remedy design and monitoring compliance:** What constitutes a "neutral" algorithm? Unlike the Microsoft remedy of disclosing interface information and licensing on non-discriminatory terms (`Lecture:442`), algorithm neutrality is harder to define objectively. Should the algorithm prioritize relevance? Consumer ratings? A combination? How should authorities monitor ongoing compliance without continuous technical oversight? Structural remedies (forcing separation of ShopLink's marketplace and seller activities) versus behavioral remedies (algorithm transparency requirements, non-discrimination obligations) present different trade-offs between effectiveness and intrusiveness.

**Final Conclusion:**

**Yes**, ShopLink's conduct likely constitutes an abuse of dominant position under Article 102 TFEU.

**Reasoning:** ShopLink is an undertaking with a likely dominant position in the EU online marketplace market, assuming market share exceeding 40% and significant entry barriers created by network effects and data advantages. Its algorithmic self-preferencing constitutes exclusionary abuse under Article 102(b) and (c), harming both competing sellers (through reduced visibility and sales) and consumers (through less relevant search results and reduced innovation incentives). The conduct affects a substantial part of the internal market given ShopLink's EU-wide operations. While ShopLink might argue justifications based on quality control, efficiency gains, or consumer preference, the "regardless of relevance or consumer ratings" nature of the algorithmic prioritization indicates the conduct is disproportionate and not objectively justified. The practice mirrors the exclusionary effects seen in the Microsoft case, where foreclosing competitors from effective market access was deemed abusive. However, enforcement authorities would face significant practical challenges including algorithmic opacity, market definition complexity, evidence gathering difficulties, the mismatch between enforcement speed and algorithm changes, and remedy design challenges.
