## Question 1 — Module 1: Regulatory Strategies and Data Platform Regulation (25 points)

### Question 1a (9 points)

*Define and explain the core mechanisms of command-and-control, disclosure, and risk-based regulatory strategies. For each strategy, identify the key accountability levers and information requirements.*

**Answer:**

*   **Command-and-Control (C&C) Regulation:**
    *   **Mechanism:** This is a direct form of state regulation where the government imposes specific, uniform standards of conduct and enforces them with sanctions, often criminal penalties. It involves setting prescriptive rules (e.g., "Thou shalt not emit more than X amount of pollutant") that apply to all regulated entities.
    *   **Accountability Levers:** Accountability is primarily enforced through state-led monitoring, inspections, and legal prosecution for non-compliance. The state is directly accountable to the public for setting and enforcing protective standards.
    *   **Information Requirements:** This strategy has very high information requirements for the regulator. To set effective standards, the regulator needs detailed technical and economic information about the regulated industry, which can be costly to acquire and may lead to reliance on industry data, creating a risk of regulatory capture.

*   **Disclosure Regulation:**
    *   **Mechanism:** This strategy aims to influence behavior by mandating the provision of information. Instead of prescribing conduct, it requires entities to be transparent about their products, services, or processes (e.g., food nutritional labels, fuel efficiency ratings). The assumption is that informed consumers or the public will make better choices, creating market pressure for desirable outcomes.
    *   **Accountability Levers:** Accountability operates through market and social mechanisms. Companies are accountable to consumers, who can "vote with their wallets." Regulators are accountable for ensuring the accuracy and clarity of the disclosed information. "Naming and shaming" by regulators can also be a powerful lever.
    *   **Information Requirements:** The primary information requirement is on the regulated entity to produce and disclose the information. The regulator's role is to define what must be disclosed and in what format, and to police the accuracy of this information, which is generally less burdensome than the requirements for C&C.

*   **Risk-Based Regulation:**
    *   **Mechanism:** This strategy involves the regulator prioritizing its attention and resources based on an assessment of the biggest risks. Instead of applying uniform rules to all, it tailors the intensity of regulation to the level of risk posed by an activity or entity. It requires developing risk-scoring mechanisms to identify and focus on high-risk areas.
    *   **Accountability Levers:** The regulator is accountable for its risk assessment methodology and its effectiveness in preventing harm. It must justify why some risks are prioritized over others. Regulated entities in high-risk categories are subject to more intense scrutiny and are accountable for managing their specific risks.
    *   **Information Requirements:** This strategy requires significant information and cooperation from the industry for the regulator to accurately assess risks. It also demands considerable expertise within the regulatory body to develop and maintain credible risk-assessment models.

**Mark Scheme (9 points):**
- **C&C:** 1 point for mechanism, 1 for accountability, 1 for information requirements. (3 points)
- **Disclosure:** 1 point for mechanism, 1 for accountability, 1 for information requirements. (3 points)
- **Risk-Based:** 1 point for mechanism, 1 for accountability, 1 for information requirements. (3 points)

### Question 1b (6 points)

*The Digital Services Act (DSA) imposes disclosure requirements regarding algorithmic content moderation and recommendation algorithms on large platforms, combined with risk-based assessments for "very large online platforms." Explain how the DSA combines disclosure and risk-based strategies. What are the strengths and limitations of this combined approach?*

**Answer:**

The DSA combines disclosure and risk-based strategies in a layered approach.

*   **Combination of Strategies:**
    1.  **Disclosure (Baseline for all):** The DSA mandates transparency and disclosure obligations for all intermediary services. For instance, platforms must be transparent about their content moderation practices and the main parameters used in their recommender systems. This empowers users and researchers by providing them with crucial information.
    2.  **Risk-Based (Intensified for VLOPs):** For "Very Large Online Platforms" (VLOPs) and "Very Large Online Search Engines" (VLOSEs)—those with over 45 million users in the EU—the DSA applies a risk-based approach. These platforms are considered to pose higher systemic risks (e.g., dissemination of illegal content, impact on fundamental rights). They are required to conduct annual risk assessments, identify systemic risks, and implement reasonable and effective mitigation measures. They are also subject to independent audits and greater regulatory oversight.

*   **Strengths of the Combined Approach:**
    *   **Proportionality and Efficiency:** It avoids a one-size-fits-all approach, which could stifle smaller platforms with excessive compliance costs. It focuses regulatory resources on the largest players where the potential for societal harm is greatest.
    *   **Comprehensive Oversight:** The baseline disclosure rules ensure a minimum level of transparency across the board, while the risk-based rules provide in-depth oversight for high-impact platforms.
    *   **Adaptability:** A risk-based framework is more adaptable to evolving technologies and emerging harms than a purely prescriptive C&C regime.

*   **Limitations of the Combined Approach:**
    *   **Complexity:** The tiered system is complex to administer and enforce. Defining and assessing "systemic risks" is challenging and can be subjective.
    *   **Information Asymmetry:** Regulators are still heavily dependent on the VLOPs to provide accurate data for risk assessments, creating potential for capture or manipulation.
    *   **Potential for Gaps:** Harms can still arise from smaller platforms that "fly under the radar" of the intensified risk-based scrutiny. A platform just below the 45-million-user threshold could still pose significant risks.

**Mark Scheme (6 points):**
- **Explanation of Combination:** 2 points for clearly explaining the dual mechanism (disclosure for all, risk-based for VLOPs).
- **Strengths:** 2 points for identifying at least two valid strengths (e.g., proportionality, efficiency, adaptability).
- **Limitations:** 2 points for identifying at least two valid limitations (e.g., complexity, information asymmetry, gaps).

### Question 1c (6 points)

*Discuss the concept of "regulatory disconnect" as presented by Brownsword. Identify and explain at least three variants of regulatory disconnect that could arise in regulating health-monitoring wearables. How does the "pacing problem" contribute to regulatory disconnect?*

**Answer:**

"Regulatory disconnect," as described by Roger Brownsword, refers to the widening gap between a regulatory framework, often designed for a past technological landscape, and the new realities created by rapid technological innovation. The "pacing problem" is the core driver of this disconnect, where the slow pace of legal and regulatory development cannot keep up with the fast pace of technological change.

In the context of health-monitoring wearables, several variants of regulatory disconnect could arise:

1.  **Regulatory Voids/Gaps:** This occurs when a new technology (like an AI-driven health recommendation algorithm) emerges for which no specific regulation exists. For example, if a wearable's AI provides a dangerously incorrect health recommendation, it might not be clear whether this falls under medical device regulation, consumer protection law, or if it exists in a legal grey area.
2.  **Ambiguity in Application:** Existing rules may be difficult to apply to the new technology. For instance, is the continuous collection of physiological data by a consumer-grade wearable governed by the same rules as data collected in a clinical setting? The GDPR applies, but its application to data processed *on-device* versus *in the cloud* can be ambiguous.
3.  **Regulatory Obsolescence/Under-regulation:** The existing rules are formally applicable but substantively inadequate. A regulation designed for traditional medical devices might not account for the risks of a device that is constantly learning and updating its algorithm based on user data. The rules become obsolete because they fail to address the novel risks (e.g., algorithmic bias, data security for continuous streams of sensitive data).

The **pacing problem** directly contributes to these disconnects. Wearable technology and AI evolve much faster than legislative cycles. By the time a law is passed to regulate a specific feature (e.g., a certain type of sensor), the technology has already moved on to new features (e.g., predictive diagnostics based on novel biomarkers), creating a perpetual state of catch-up for regulators and leading to the disconnects described above.

**Mark Scheme (6 points):**
- **Definition of Disconnect/Pacing Problem:** 2 points for a clear explanation of regulatory disconnect and the role of the pacing problem.
- **Identification of Variants:** 3 points for identifying and clearly explaining three distinct variants in the context of wearables (1 point per variant).
- **Linkage:** 1 point for explicitly linking the pacing problem to the emergence of these disconnects.

### Question 1d (4 points)

*Propose a regulatory mix for the health-monitoring wearables described above. Your proposal should combine at least three different strategies. Justify your choices by explaining how each strategy addresses specific market failures or rights-based concerns, and discuss any trade-offs between the strategies you've chosen.*

**Answer:**

A suitable regulatory mix for health-monitoring wearables would combine several strategies to balance innovation, safety, and user rights.

1.  **Risk-Based Regulation (for AI and Health Claims):** The core AI algorithms that provide health recommendations should be subject to a risk-based approach, similar to the AI Act.
    *   **Justification:** This addresses the risk of harm from inaccurate or biased advice. Systems making high-stakes recommendations (e.g., suggesting changes in medication intake) would face stringent requirements for data quality, testing, and human oversight. This is more flexible than C&C, allowing for innovation while focusing scrutiny where it's most needed.
2.  **Disclosure Regulation (for Data Practices):** Companies must provide clear, simple, and accessible information about what data is collected, how it is used, who it is shared with, and the main parameters of the AI's decision-making.
    *   **Justification:** This addresses the significant information asymmetry between manufacturers and users, which is a key market failure. It empowers users to make informed choices, addressing the rights-based concern of data privacy and autonomy.
3.  **Command-and-Control (for Baseline Safety and Security):** A set of non-negotiable, prescriptive rules should apply to all devices.
    *   **Justification:** This is necessary for fundamental protections. For example, C&C rules could mandate minimum data encryption standards to prevent breaches (addressing security risks) and prohibit the sale of user health data to third-party data brokers without explicit consent (addressing the fundamental right to privacy).

**Trade-offs:**
The main trade-off is between the certainty and protection of C&C and the flexibility of risk-based and disclosure strategies. Over-reliance on C&C could stifle innovation in this fast-moving field. Conversely, relying solely on disclosure assumes a level of user engagement and understanding that may not exist, and a purely risk-based approach might miss novel risks until after harm has occurred. This proposed mix aims to balance these trade-offs by using C&C for fundamental red lines, risk-based regulation for the most complex and high-impact components (the AI), and disclosure to empower users.

### Question 2a (8 points)

*Applying Articles 51, 53, and 54 of the EHDS Regulation, assess whether each of the five data types listed above should be made available for secondary use. For each data type, explain your reasoning by reference to the permitted purposes, typical exclusions, and relevant safeguards under the EHDS.*

**Answer:**

The EHDS Regulation establishes a framework for the secondary use of electronic health data. The general principle is to make a broad range of health data available for purposes beneficial to society, subject to strict conditions and prohibitions.

1.  **Electronic Health Records (EHRs):** **Yes.** Article 51 explicitly lists EHRs as a minimum category of data to be made available for secondary use. This includes patient summaries, lab results, and discharge reports. These are core clinical data essential for most health research.
2.  **Data from wellness applications:** **Yes.** Article 51 also includes "data from medical devices, wellness applications and other digital health applications" as a category to be made available. This is crucial for understanding lifestyle factors in health research.
3.  **Regional cancer registry data:** **Yes.** Data from health-related registries, such as disease-specific registries (e.g., cancer), are explicitly included under Article 51. This data is vital for epidemiological studies and public health policy-making.
4.  **Medical imaging data (X-rays and MRI scans):** **Yes.** Medical imaging data is a key component of EHRs and is listed as a priority category for exchange. Its availability for secondary use is essential for developing and training AI diagnostic tools, as in Project A.
5.  **Administrative data on healthcare costs and insurance reimbursements:** **Yes.** Article 51 includes "administrative data, such as claims and reimbursement data." This data is permitted for secondary use for purposes like policy-making, statistics, or research into healthcare system efficiency.

**Reasoning:** All five data types fall under the minimum categories listed in Article 51 of the EHDS Regulation. Their availability is presumed for permitted purposes under Article 53, such as scientific research, development and innovation activities, policy-making, and regulatory activities. Access would be granted via a permit from a Health Data Access Body (HDAB) and processed in a secure processing environment, ensuring data minimisation and privacy safeguards.

### Question 3b (7 points)

*Analyze the exclusive dealing agreements and MFN clauses under Article 101 TFEU. Your analysis should cover: (i) whether there is an agreement between undertakings; (ii) whether the agreement restricts competition by object or effect; (iii) whether the agreement appreciably affects trade between Member States; (iv) whether the agreement could qualify for an exemption under Article 101(3).*

**Answer:**

*   **(i) Agreement between Undertakings:** **Yes.** HealthNet and the hospital chains are separate "undertakings" (entities engaged in economic activity). The contracts between them constitute "agreements" under Article 101.

*   **(ii) Restriction of Competition:**
    *   **Exclusive Dealing:** These agreements, which require hospitals to use only HealthNet, have the **effect** of restricting competition. They foreclose a significant portion of the market (the top 10 hospital chains) from HealthNet's competitors, making it difficult for rivals to enter or survive.
    *   **MFN Clauses:** These "Most-Favored Nation" clauses also restrict competition by **effect**. They reduce the hospitals' incentive to offer better terms to a new entrant (as they would have to pass those terms on to the dominant HealthNet), thereby softening competition and potentially leading to higher prices across the market. They can act as a barrier to entry for more efficient or innovative competitors.
    *   Neither is likely a restriction by *object*, but their anti-competitive *effects* are strong.

*   **(iii) Effect on Trade between Member States:** **Yes.** The agreements involve a platform operating "across the EU" and the "top 10 private hospital chains in the EU." Such agreements, by partitioning the market and foreclosing competitors who operate or wish to operate across borders, have an appreciable effect on trade between Member States.

*   **(iv) Exemption under Article 101(3):** **Unlikely.** For an exemption, the agreements must (1) contribute to improving production/distribution or promoting technical/economic progress, (2) allow consumers a fair share of the benefit, (3) be indispensable, and (4) not eliminate competition.
    *   HealthNet might argue the agreements create efficiencies. However, it is highly unlikely that total exclusivity (exclusive dealing) or the MFN clauses are **indispensable** to achieve these efficiencies. Furthermore, by foreclosing competitors and softening price competition, they likely harm consumers and move towards **eliminating competition**, failing the last two conditions.

### Question 3c (6 points)

*Analyze the bundling practice and refusal to provide data under Article 102 TFEU. Apply the relevant tests for abuse of dominance, including consideration of the separate products test for tying, foreclosure effects, and the essential facilities doctrine for refusal to supply. Discuss any objective justifications HealthNet might raise.*

**Answer:**

As a dominant undertaking, HealthNet has a special responsibility not to allow its conduct to impair competition.

*   **Bundling/Tying:** This is an abuse of a dominant position.
    *   **Test for Tying:**
        1.  *Tying and Tied Products:* The tying product is the popular appointment scheduling module (where HealthNet is dominant). The tied products are the other services (EHR, billing, etc.). These are **separate products**, as hospitals might want to purchase them from different suppliers.
        2.  *Coercion:* HealthNet **coerces** customers by requiring them to buy the tied products to get the tying product.
        3.  *Foreclosure Effect:* This practice forecloses competitors in the markets for EHR, billing, etc., as they are shut out from competing for the custom of HealthNet's large user base.
    *   **Objective Justification:** HealthNet might argue the bundle is necessary for technical integration or efficiency. However, this is a weak argument if the products can be and are offered separately by other vendors, and interoperability is technically feasible.

*   **Refusal to Provide Data (Refusal to Supply):** This is also likely an abuse.
    *   **Essential Facilities Doctrine Test:**
        1.  *Control of Essential Facility:* The anonymized patient flow data is controlled by HealthNet.
        2.  *Indispensability:* Access to this data appears **indispensable** for a competitor (MediConnect) to compete effectively, as it's needed to optimize scheduling algorithms. It is not economically viable to replicate this data.
        3.  *Elimination of Competition:* The refusal is likely to **eliminate competition** in the downstream market for telemedicine/scheduling services.
        4.  *No Objective Justification:* HealthNet's justification (proprietary business information) is generally not sufficient to justify a refusal to supply an essential facility, especially when it eliminates all competition. The *Microsoft* case established that dominant firms may have a duty to supply interoperability information.

**Mark Scheme (6 points):**
- **Bundling/Tying:** 3 points for correctly applying the tying test (separate products, coercion, foreclosure) and concluding it's an abuse.
- **Refusal to Supply:** 3 points for correctly applying the essential facilities test (indispensable, elimination of competition, no objective justification) and concluding it's an abuse.
