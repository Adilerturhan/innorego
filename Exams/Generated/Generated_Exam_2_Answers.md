### **Question 1: Regulatory Theory & Innovation (Module 1) [25 Marks]**

**a) Explain how Synth-V exemplifies the "pacing problem" and could lead to a "regulatory disconnect." (8 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 4 marks for explaining the pacing problem, 4 marks for explaining regulatory disconnect.
*   **Answer:**
    *   **Pacing Problem:** Synth-V exemplifies the pacing problem because the technology's capability (creating realistic video from text) is advancing far more rapidly than the law can adapt. While developers can release new versions quickly, the legislative process to address the risks (e.g., misinformation, fraud) is slow and deliberative. This creates a period where a powerful, potentially harmful technology exists in a regulatory vacuum.
    *   **Regulatory Disconnect:** This pacing problem leads to a "regulatory disconnect." Existing laws, such as those for defamation or fraud, were created for a "technological landscape of the past" and may be ill-equipped to handle the scale and nature of AI-generated misinformation. For example, laws may focus on the liability of a human creator, which becomes ambiguous when content is generated by an AI. This gap between the technology's function and the law's assumptions is the regulatory disconnect.

**b) Propose and contrast two distinct regulatory strategies the EU could adopt to govern Synth-V. For each strategy, describe its main features and how it would be applied in this context. (10 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 5 marks for each well-described and contrasted strategy.
*   **Answer:**
    *   **Strategy 1: Command-and-Control.** This strategy would involve setting strict, mandatory rules. For Synth-V, this could mean an outright ban on creating deepfakes of real people without explicit consent, or a requirement that all AI-generated video be digitally watermarked as "synthetic." Enforcement would be backed by significant fines or criminal sanctions for non-compliance. It is protective but can be rigid and may become outdated quickly.
    *   **Strategy 2: Risk-Based Regulation combined with Disclosure.** This is a more nuanced approach, similar to the AI Act. The regulation would classify uses of Synth-V based on risk. Creating a deepfake for a film (with consent) would be low-risk. Using it to create political misinformation would be unacceptable or high-risk and subject to strict obligations. A core component would be **disclosure**: a mandatory rule that any public-facing synthetic video must be clearly labeled as such, empowering the public to distinguish real from fake. This is more flexible than command-and-control but requires complex risk assessments.

**c) Argue which of your two proposed strategies is superior for governing Synth-V. Your answer must justify your choice by balancing the need for "regulation of innovation" with the goal of "regulation for innovativeness." (7 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 7 marks for a well-justified argument that balances the two regulatory goals.
*   **Answer:** The **Risk-Based Regulation with Disclosure** strategy is superior.
    *   **Regulation of Innovation (Addressing Risks):** It effectively addresses the most serious risks by prohibiting or heavily restricting high-risk applications like political deepfakes, thus protecting fundamental rights and public discourse. The disclosure requirement directly counters the core harm of misinformation by removing the element of deception.
    *   **Regulation for Innovativeness (Fostering Potential):** Unlike a rigid command-and-control approach that might stifle creativity, this strategy allows beneficial, low-risk uses to flourish. The film, art, and gaming industries could use Synth-V to innovate without facing a blanket ban. It creates a predictable legal environment that encourages investment in responsible AI development. It strikes a better balance, regulating the *use* of the technology rather than the technology itself, which is key to fostering innovation while mitigating harm.

### **Question 2: Health Data Regulation (Module 2) [20 Marks]**

**a) For each of the three data types requested by Cardia-AI, identify whether it is a category of electronic health data that must be made available for secondary use under Article 51 of the EHDS proposal. (6 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 2 marks for each correct identification.
*   **Answer:**
    1.  **Patient-generated data from wellness applications:** Yes, this is explicitly listed as a category in Article 51.
    2.  **Electronic Health Records (EHRs):** Yes, EHRs are a core category of data intended for secondary use.
    3.  **Genomic data:** Yes, this is also explicitly listed as a priority category for secondary use.

**b) Cardia-AI states its purpose is "scientific research" and "development and innovation activities." Are these permitted purposes for secondary use under Article 53 of the EHDS proposal? (4 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 4 marks for confirming both are permitted purposes.
*   **Answer:** **Yes.** Article 53 explicitly lists both "scientific research related to the health or care sectors" and "development and innovation activities for products and services that contribute to public health or social security" as permitted purposes for secondary use.

**c) A patient advocacy group objects, arguing that allowing a for-profit company like Cardia-AI to use the data to develop a commercial product is a prohibited purpose. Based on Article 54, is their objection likely to succeed? Explain your reasoning. (6 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 2 marks for "No." 4 marks for the explanation.
*   **Answer:** **No,** the objection is unlikely to succeed. Article 54 prohibits using data for purposes that are detrimental to individuals (e.g., adjusting insurance premiums) or for marketing. It does **not** prohibit the development of a commercial product, especially if that product contributes to public health, as a diagnostic tool would. The EHDS framework is designed to foster innovation, including by commercial entities, provided the purpose is beneficial to society and not harmful to individuals.

**d) If a patient had previously exercised a right to opt-out of their data being used for research, must the HDAB exclude their data from the set provided to Cardia-AI? (4 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 4 marks for a clear "Yes."
*   **Answer:** **Yes.** The EHDS proposal includes a right for natural persons to opt-out of the secondary use of their health data. If a patient has exercised this right, the Health Data Access Body (HDAB) is obligated to respect that choice and must exclude that patient's data from any dataset provided for secondary use.

### **Question 3: Competition Law (Module 3) [20 Marks]**

**a) Analyse the agreement between CloudForce and ServerStack under Article 101 TFEU. Identify the type of agreement and explain whether it would be considered a restriction of competition by 'object' or 'effect'. (10 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 4 marks for identifying it as a vertical agreement. 6 marks for correctly analyzing it as a restriction by effect.
*   **Answer:**
    *   **Legal Issue:** The agreement between CloudForce (buyer) and ServerStack (supplier) is a **vertical agreement**, as they operate at different levels of the supply chain.
    *   **Analysis:** This agreement likely constitutes an infringement of Article 101 TFEU. It is not a hardcore restriction by 'object' (like price-fixing). Instead, it would be assessed based on its **'effect'** on competition. The effect is to foreclose a competitor (DataSpark) from accessing essential inputs (high-performance servers), which restricts DataSpark's ability to compete with CloudForce. This weakens competition in the cloud computing market, leading to less choice and potentially higher prices for consumers. The agreement has the clear effect of preventing, restricting, or distorting competition.

**b) Analyse CloudForce's pricing strategy under Article 102 TFEU. Identify the key elements required to establish an abuse of a dominant position in this scenario. (10 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 10 marks for identifying and applying the three key elements.
*   **Answer:**
    *   **Legal Issue:** CloudForce's pricing strategy is a potential abuse of a dominant position under Article 102 TFEU. The specific practice is known as **predatory pricing**.
    *   **Analysis (Elements):**
        1.  **Dominant Position:** CloudForce holds an 80% market share, which is a clear indicator of dominance in the relevant market (EU cloud computing infrastructure).
        2.  **Abuse:** The abuse is the practice of predatory pricing—selling services below cost. This is not a normal form of competition. Its purpose is to harm competitors by making it impossible for them to compete on price without also incurring unsustainable losses.
        3.  **Effect on Competition:** The likely effect is the foreclosure of the market to competitors like DataSpark. By driving them out of business, CloudForce can later recoup its losses by raising prices in a market with little to no competition, ultimately harming consumers. This conduct is an abuse of its special responsibility as a dominant firm not to impair genuine, undistorted competition.

### **Question 4: Intellectual Property & Data (Module 4) [15 Marks]**

**a) Is Astrid's database likely to be protected by **copyright** under the Database Directive (96/9/EC)? Justify your answer by applying the 'Author's Own Intellectual Creation' (AOIC) standard to the selection or arrangement of the database's contents. (7 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 2 marks for "No." 5 marks for applying the AOIC standard correctly.
*   **Answer:** **No,** the database is unlikely to be protected by copyright. Copyright protection requires the **selection or arrangement** of the contents to be the "Author's Own Intellectual Creation" (AOIC).
    *   **Selection:** Astrid did not creatively *select* the data; she aimed to record *every* visible meteor. This is a comprehensive, not a creative, selection.
    *   **Arrangement:** The arrangement (chronological and by celestial quadrant) is standard and functional for astronomical data. It is dictated by technical and logical considerations, not by free, creative choices. Therefore, the database lacks the originality and "personal touch" of the author required to meet the AOIC standard.

**b) Is Astrid's database likely to qualify for the ***sui generis* database right**? Justify your answer by analyzing whether she has made a "substantial investment" in the obtaining, verification, or presentation of the data, and explain the key distinction between investment in *creating* data versus *obtaining* data. (8 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 2 marks for "No." 6 marks for correctly analyzing the investment and the create/obtain distinction.
*   **Answer:** **No,** the database is unlikely to qualify for the *sui generis* right.
    *   **Substantial Investment:** The right protects substantial investment in the **obtaining, verification, or presentation** of the contents of a database.
    *   **Creation vs. Obtaining:** The crucial distinction is between *creating* data and *obtaining* existing data. Astrid's effort and time were invested in **creating** the data (i.e., generating new observations through her own activity of watching the sky). The *sui generis* right is not intended to protect the investment in creating the data itself, but rather the investment in collecting, verifying, or presenting pre-existing data. Because her database is a "spin-off" of her primary activity of astronomical observation, the investment is considered to be in data creation, not obtaining. Therefore, it does not qualify for the *sui generis* right.

### **Question 5: AI, Data Governance & Ethics (Module 5) [20 Marks]**

**a) Under the proposed EU AI Act, would the FairPark system likely be classified as "high-risk"? Explain your reasoning. (5 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 2 marks for "Yes." 3 marks for the reasoning.
*   **Answer:** **Yes,** the FairPark system would likely be classified as "high-risk." Annex III of the AI Act lists AI systems intended for **law enforcement** purposes as high-risk. Since this system is used to dispatch enforcement officers and predict violations, it falls squarely within that category. It has a direct impact on the administration of justice and the rights of individuals.

**b) Identify and explain two distinct types of bias (e.g., selection, reporting, historical) that are likely present in the training data, causing the skewed enforcement predictions. (8 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 4 marks for each correctly identified and explained bias.
*   **Answer:**
    1.  **Historical Bias:** The training data consists of parking fines from the last five years. If, historically, enforcement officers have patrolled low-income neighbourhoods more frequently than affluent ones (due to pre-existing biases, resource allocation, or other factors), the dataset will reflect this. The AI learns this skewed pattern and incorrectly concludes that more violations occur in those areas, thus perpetuating the historical inequality in enforcement.
    2.  **Selection Bias (or Sampling Bias):** The data was collected by scraping a traffic app. The users of this specific app may not be representative of the entire driving population. For example, if the app is more popular among younger drivers or those in certain geographic areas, the data will be skewed towards their behaviours, leading to a model that is not representative of the city as a whole.

**c) From a data governance perspective under Article 10 of the AI Act, identify at least two requirements the municipality failed to meet when creating and using their training data. (7 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 3.5 marks for each correctly identified failure.
*   **Answer:**
    1.  **Lawful Data Collection:** The data was obtained by scraping an app in violation of its terms of service. This is not a lawful method of data collection. Data governance requires that data be collected in a legally compliant manner.
    2.  **Data Representativeness and Freedom from Bias:** Article 10 requires that training data be "sufficiently relevant, representative, and free of errors and complete." The municipality failed this requirement. The data was not representative of the actual distribution of parking violations across all neighbourhoods (as evidenced by the skewed results) and was contaminated with historical bias. They did not perform an adequate examination for possible biases before training the model.
