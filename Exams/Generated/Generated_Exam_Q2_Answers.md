## **Case Background**

**Zanopi** is one of the largest pharmaceutical manufacturers in Europe, currently developing a novel treatment for mood stabilization and emotional well-being, specifically for personalized treatment of patients with bipolar disorder. To conduct scientific research for this new medicine, Zanopi requires access to large amounts of high-quality health data. Zanopi identifies several mental health clinics in **Nederlux** (an EU Member State) that specialize in treating bipolar disorder and store their patients' data in **Electronic Health Records (EHRs)**. Zanopi's researchers believe that EHR data would be highly valuable for research and development, as it includes structured information about health histories, diagnoses, interventions, and treatment outcomes.

---

### **Question 1: Secondary Use of Health Data (Module 2) [2 Marks]**

**Would Zanopi accessing and processing electronic health data from EHRs for drug research and development qualify as secondary use of health data under the EHDS Regulation? Explain why or why not. (2 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 1 mark for defining secondary use, 1 mark for applying to Zanopi facts.
*   **Answer:**
    *   **Yes**, Zanopi's proposed use qualifies as **secondary use of health data** under the EHDS Regulation.

        **Definition**: **Secondary use** refers to processing electronic health data for purposes other than individual healthcare provision—specifically for research, innovation, policy-making, regulatory activities, education, patient safety, or personalized medicine. This contrasts with **primary use** (healthcare delivery, treatment, prescription, administrative services) (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:22-29`).

        **Application to Zanopi**:
        *   **Original purpose**: EHR data from Nederlux mental health clinics was collected for **primary use**—treating patients with bipolar disorder
        *   **Proposed purpose**: Zanopi wants to **re-use** this data for drug R&D, a fundamentally different purpose
        *   **Legal classification**: Article 51 includes **"electronic health data from EHRs"** as a category for secondary use (`Module_2/OJ_L_202500327_EN_TXT.md:1433`)
        *   **Permitted purpose**: Article 53(1)(e) lists **"scientific research related to health or care sectors"** and **"development and innovation activities for products or services"** as permitted purposes (`Module_2/OJ_L_202500327_EN_TXT.md:1494-1495`)
        *   **Fits definition**: Developing a novel bipolar disorder treatment clearly falls within permitted secondary use purposes

        **Conclusion**: Since Zanopi's purpose (drug R&D) differs from the original clinical care purpose and is explicitly listed as permitted secondary use under Article 53, this constitutes **secondary use**. Though Zanopi is commercial, Article 53(1)(e) permits development and innovation activities contributing to public health regardless of entity type, subject to **data permit system** managed by HDABs ensuring necessity, proportionality, and data protection compliance.

### **Question 2: Use of EHR Data for Marketing (Module 2) [1 Mark]**

**Zanopi's executives suggest using this EHR data for marketing purposes, such as identifying potential candidates for the new treatment and advertising it to them. Does the EHDS Regulation allow this? Explain your answer. (1 mark)**

*   **Marks:** 1 mark for "No" with explanation.
*   **Answer:**
    *   **No**, the EHDS Regulation does **not** allow this use.

        **Legal prohibition**: Article 54 explicitly **prohibits** using health data for **"advertising or marketing activities"** (`Module_2/OJ_L_202500327_EN_TXT.md:1514`). Zanopi's proposed activity—using EHR data to identify potential candidates and advertise the treatment—directly constitutes prohibited marketing.

        **Rationale**: The EHDS framework enables beneficial secondary uses (scientific research) while protecting individuals from commercial exploitation of sensitive health data. Marketing to individuals identified through health data analysis violates this protective boundary.

        **Enforcement**: Violations face **administrative fines up to €20,000,000 or 4% of worldwide annual turnover** under Article 64 (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:56`). HDABs can revoke data permits or exclude users from EHDS access for up to five years.

        **Key distinction**: While Zanopi may legitimately access data for research under Article 53(1)(e), repurposing that data for marketing crosses into prohibited territory under Article 54(c), reflecting the fundamental tension between enabling innovation and preventing exploitation.

### **Question 3: Reasons for Regulating Secondary Use of Health Data (Module 2) [2 Marks]**

**Identify and explain the main reasons for regulating secondary use of health data in the EHDS Regulation. In your answer, highlight both the expected benefits and challenges that justified regulatory intervention. (2 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 1 mark for expected benefits, 1 mark for challenges.
*   **Answer:**
    *   The EHDS regulates secondary use to balance **expected benefits** against **challenges** that market mechanisms alone cannot resolve.

        **Expected Benefits:**

        1. **Accelerated research and innovation**:
            *   Faster drug development (like Zanopi's bipolar disorder treatment)
            *   Rare disease research requiring multi-country data aggregation
            *   Pharmacovigilance from diverse adverse event reports
            *   Medical device innovation through real-world evidence
            *   **Economies of scope**: data collected for clinical care re-used at low marginal cost
            *   **Economies of scale**: aggregation dramatically increases statistical power (`Module_2/Generated/consolidated_module_2_summary.md:50-56`)

        2. **Improved public health and policy**:
            *   Evidence-based policymaking for health interventions
            *   Disease surveillance and outbreak response (COVID-19 demonstrated necessity)
            *   Health technology assessment strengthened by comparative effectiveness data

        3. **Overcoming market failures**:
            *   Voluntary sharing mechanisms proved insufficient
            *   Health data remained fragmented across incompatible national systems
            *   Cross-Border Healthcare Directive's failure demonstrated need for regulation (`Module_2/Generated/consolidated_module_2_summary.md:39-43`)

        **Challenges Justifying Intervention:**

        1. **Fundamental rights protection**:
            *   Health data reveals sensitive information (physical, mental, genetic, behavioral characteristics)
            *   EU Charter fundamental rights: privacy (Article 7), data protection (Article 8), human dignity
            *   Mental health data carries heightened stigma/discrimination risks (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:92-93`)

        2. **Risk of exploitation and misuse**:
            *   Prohibited marketing to vulnerable individuals
            *   Insurance discrimination based on health predictions
            *   Re-identification risks despite anonymization efforts
            *   Addressed through Article 54 prohibitions, mandatory data permits, enforcement mechanisms

        3. **Public trust and value leakage**:
            *   Broad sharing without control could erode patient trust in healthcare systems and medical research
            *   **"Value leakage"**: public health data benefits private corporations (training proprietary algorithms) without public return
            *   Potential scenario: public health systems buy back services built on their own data (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:95`)

        **Conclusion**: The EHDS creates a framework unlocking innovation benefits (enabling companies like Zanopi to develop treatments) while establishing mandatory safeguards—HDABs, data permits, prohibited purposes, secure processing environments, high penalties—that markets alone would not provide.

### **Question 4: Patient Rights and Interests (Module 2) [3 Marks]**

**The association representing patients with mental health problems in Nederlux, MINT, expresses concerns about sharing health data with pharmaceutical companies. Under the EHDS Regulation, do patients have any way to oppose their health data being shared for secondary use? Explain. Overall, do you consider the interests of citizens adequately recognized and upheld by the EHDS Regulation in cases of secondary use? Elaborate and justify your answer. (3 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 1 mark for explaining opt-out right, 2 marks for adequacy assessment with balanced justification.
*   **Answer:**
    *   **Part 1: Can patients oppose secondary use?**

        **Yes**, through the **opt-out right**. Natural persons can opt out from secondary processing:
        *   **At any time**, reversibly, **without providing a reason** (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:57`)
        *   **Effect**: HDABs must exclude that patient's data from datasets provided for secondary use
        *   **Application**: Mental health patients represented by MINT can prevent data sharing with Zanopi by opting out

        **Important limitation—Public interest override**:
        *   Member States **may** override opt-out if data needed by **public sector bodies** for key public interest purposes (serious cross-border health threats or scientific research for important public interest) (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:58`)
        *   **Restrictive conditions**: Data cannot be obtained by alternative means, processing necessary and proportionate, justification required per application (`Module_2/Generated/consolidated_module_2_summary.md:504-522`)
        *   **Application to Zanopi**: Since Zanopi is **commercial** (not public sector), override would **not** apply—patient opt-out remains effective

        **Part 2: Are citizen interests adequately recognized?**

        **Assessment**: **Partially adequate**—significant protections exist but serious concerns remain.

        **Arguments for adequacy**:
        *   **Data permit systems**: Rigorous HDAB assessment for legitimacy, proportionality, necessity
        *   **Prohibited purposes** (Article 54): Marketing, insurance discrimination, harmful products, re-identification banned
        *   **Secure Processing Environments**: Controlled access settings
        *   **Data minimization**: Anonymization preferred; otherwise pseudonymization with re-identification keys held only by HDABs
        *   **High penalties**: €20M or 4% turnover; permit revocation; five-year user exclusion
        *   **Opt-out right**: Direct individual control

        **Arguments against adequacy**:
        *   **Erosion of control**: Opt-out (vs. consent) "lowers standards for information provision and diminishes individual control" (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:93`); data shared unless patients actively object; many may be unaware or unable to make informed decisions
        *   **Public interest override**: Can negate opt-out rights with somewhat ambiguous boundaries
        *   **Public trust risk**: "Broad access could backfire and harm trust if perceived as high-risk without clear benefits" (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:94`)
        *   **Value leakage**: Tech corporations gain access; concerns about "proprietary algorithms trained on public data sold back to public health systems" (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:95`)
        *   **Implementation burden**: "Unsustainable administrative burden on HDABs; inadequate incentives for health professionals" (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:96`)

        **Conclusion**: While the EHDS establishes important protections, the shift from consent to opt-out, combined with public interest overrides and broad commercial access potential (like Zanopi's), creates legitimate concerns about individual control erosion. For mental health patients (MINT's constituency), where sensitivity is exceptionally high and stigma/discrimination risks significant, opt-out default may be insufficient. A more balanced approach would require: enhanced transparency, narrower override criteria, stronger value return mechanisms, and differentiated governance for highly sensitive data categories potentially requiring opt-in consent.

### **Question 5: Cross-Border Healthcare Data Access (Module 2) [2 Marks]**

**Mr. Barker is a patient in one of the clinics identified by Zanopi. He is planning to move from Nederlux to another EU Member State, where he would like to continue receiving treatment for bipolar disorder. Will the doctor in Mr. Barker's new country of residence be able to access information about his past medical test results and electronic prescriptions issued in the Nederlux clinic to provide healthcare? Explain why or why not. (2 marks)**

*   **Marks:** 1 mark for "Yes" with assumptions, 1 mark for explaining MyHealth@EU framework and timelines.
*   **Answer:**
    *   **Yes**, Mr. Barker's new doctor will access his past medical test results and electronic prescriptions, **assuming** implementation timelines met and Mr. Barker has not opted out from primary use.

        **Legal framework (Primary Use—Chapter II)**:
        *   **Purpose**: Providing treatment to Mr. Barker = **primary use** (healthcare delivery), not secondary use
        *   **No data permits required**: Restrictive requirements applying to Zanopi's research access do **not** apply to cross-border clinical care
        *   **Health Professional Access Services**: Enable access to relevant patient data for continuity of care (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:46`)
        *   **Legal basis**: Article 11 provides health professionals "shall have access to relevant and necessary personal electronic health data... irrespective of the Member State" (`Module_2/OJ_L_202500327_EN_TXT.md:715-717`)

        **Infrastructure**:
        *   **MyHealth@EU**: Mandatory cross-border infrastructure for exchanging EHR data between Member States for primary use (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:31`)
        *   Facilitates automatic, secure exchange when healthcare providers need data for clinical purposes

        **Data categories and implementation timeline** (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:40, 70-72`):
        *   **Electronic prescriptions**: Core priority category → accessible from **March 2029**
        *   **Medical test results**: Complex priority category → accessible from **March 2031**
        *   **EHDS Regulation applies**: 26 March 2027 (full implementation staged)

        **Patient control**:
        *   **Primary use opt-out**: Member States may allow opt-out from EHR data access via EHDS services (Article 10) (`Smaller_Summaries/Regulation_of_Health_Data_Sharing.md:45`)
        *   **Effect if exercised**: Data not available through MyHealth@EU (though Nederlux providers maintain records nationally)
        *   **Practical likelihood**: Most patients would not opt out—doing so hinders treatment quality when moving between countries
        *   **Distinction**: Primary use opt-out (cross-border care) is separate from secondary use opt-out (research)—Mr. Barker could opt out from Zanopi's research while allowing his new doctor access

        **Conclusion**: Assuming implementation deadlines passed (March 2029 for prescriptions, March 2031 for test results), both Member States deployed MyHealth@EU infrastructure, and Mr. Barker has not opted out from primary use, his new doctor will access this information to provide continuing bipolar disorder treatment. This cross-border access is precisely what the EHDS primary use framework was designed to enable—facilitating healthcare continuity for EU citizens exercising freedom of movement while maintaining data protection through secure infrastructure, access logging, and patient control mechanisms.
