### **Question 1: Regulatory Theory & Innovation (Module 1) [25 Marks]**

A new generation of generative AI has emerged, capable of creating photorealistic video content from simple text prompts. This technology, known as "Synth-V," has immense potential for the creative industries but also poses significant risks, such as the rapid creation of convincing misinformation ("deepfakes") that could influence public opinion or commit fraud. The EU is considering how to approach this new technology.

a) Explain how Synth-V exemplifies the "pacing problem" and could lead to a "regulatory disconnect." (8 marks)

b) Propose and contrast two distinct regulatory strategies the EU could adopt to govern Synth-V. For each strategy, describe its main features and how it would be applied in this context. (10 marks)

c) Argue which of your two proposed strategies is superior for governing Synth-V. Your answer must justify your choice by balancing the need for "regulation of innovation" (addressing risks) with the goal of "regulation for innovativeness" (fostering its positive potential). (7 marks)

### **Question 2: Health Data Regulation (Module 2) [20 Marks]**

"Cardia-AI," a private research company, aims to develop a novel AI-powered diagnostic tool for early-stage heart disease. To do this, they submit a data access application to the national Health Data Access Body (HDAB). They request access to a large, pseudonymised dataset containing three types of data from public hospitals:
1.  Patient-generated data from wellness applications (e.g., daily heart rate, activity levels).
2.  Electronic Health Records (EHRs) containing official diagnoses and medication histories.
3.  Genomic data from individuals who volunteered for a previous, unrelated genetic study.

a) For each of the three data types requested by Cardia-AI, identify whether it is a category of electronic health data that must be made available for secondary use under Article 51 of the EHDS proposal. (6 marks)

b) Cardia-AI states its purpose is "scientific research" and "development and innovation activities." Are these permitted purposes for secondary use under Article 53 of the EHDS proposal? (4 marks)

c) A patient advocacy group objects, arguing that allowing a for-profit company like Cardia-AI to use the data to develop a commercial product is a prohibited purpose. Based on Article 54, is their objection likely to succeed? Explain your reasoning. (6 marks)

d) If a patient had previously exercised a right to opt-out of their data being used for research, must the HDAB exclude their data from the set provided to Cardia-AI? (4 marks)

### **Question 3: Competition Law (Module 3) [20 Marks]**

"CloudForce" is the dominant provider of cloud computing infrastructure in the EU, holding an 80% market share. "DataSpark" is a smaller, innovative competitor. To stifle competition, CloudForce implements two strategies:

1.  It enters into a secret agreement with "ServerStack," its main hardware supplier, in which ServerStack agrees not to supply its latest high-performance servers to DataSpark for the next two years.
2.  It begins offering its primary cloud storage service at prices significantly below its own costs, targeting the same customer segment as DataSpark.

a) Analyse the agreement between CloudForce and ServerStack under Article 101 TFEU. Identify the type of agreement and explain whether it would be considered a restriction of competition by 'object' or 'effect'. (10 marks)

b) Analyse CloudForce's pricing strategy under Article 102 TFEU. Identify the key elements required to establish an abuse of a dominant position in this scenario. (10 marks)

### **Question 4: Intellectual Property & Data (Module 4) [15 Marks]**

An amateur astronomer, Astrid, spends two years using her telescope to observe and record the brightness, position, and time of every visible meteor in the night sky over her city. She meticulously records this data in a notebook. She then spends a significant amount of time and effort digitizing these observations into a structured digital format, creating a comprehensive "Meteor Sighting Database." The database is organized chronologically and by celestial quadrant, but the structure itself is standard for astronomical data.

a) Is Astrid's database likely to be protected by **copyright** under the Database Directive (96/9/EC)? Justify your answer by applying the 'Author's Own Intellectual Creation' (AOIC) standard to the selection or arrangement of the database's contents. (7 marks)

b) Is Astrid's database likely to qualify for the ***sui generis* database right**? Justify your answer by analyzing whether she has made a "substantial investment" in the obtaining, verification, or presentation of the data, and explain the key distinction between investment in *creating* data versus *obtaining* data. (8 marks)

### **Question 5: AI, Data Governance & Ethics (Module 5) [20 Marks]**

A municipality launches "FairPark," an AI system to predict parking violation hotspots and dispatch enforcement officers. The system is trained on a public dataset of parking fines issued over the last five years. To create the training data, the municipality scraped location data from a popular, publicly accessible traffic app, which is against the app's terms of service. The resulting AI system disproportionately sends officers to low-income neighbourhoods, even though residents there report that illegal parking is just as common in affluent areas.

a) Under the proposed EU AI Act, would the FairPark system likely be classified as "high-risk"? Explain your reasoning. (5 marks)

b) Identify and explain two distinct types of bias (e.g., selection, reporting, historical) that are likely present in the training data, causing the skewed enforcement predictions. (8 marks)

c) From a data governance perspective under Article 10 of the AI Act, identify at least two requirements the municipality failed to meet when creating and using their training data. (7 marks)
