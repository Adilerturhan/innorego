# What this course is about

* This course explores the interaction between innovation and regulation in the field of data science.
* It draws mainly on economic theories of innovation and regulation, and focuses on data driven innovation.
* It discusses theories of innovation and regulation in general and applies them in specific fields, such as agriculture and finance.

# Course objectives

Upon completion of this course, you will have obtained a basic understanding of the fundamental theories and principles underpinning the regulation of innovation, and the interplay between regulation and innovation in general, and innovation driven by (big) data in particular.

You will be able to:

1. identify real-life examples of (data driven) innovation and evaluate the potential social and economic impact of such innovation;
2. identify and understand the role of economic, social and rights-based considerations as grounds for regulation of innovative technologies;
3. identify different types of regulatory strategies and regimes applicable to innovation regulation, including risk-based strategies;
4. rely on your knowledge of regulatory regimes to inform decisions in engineering design;
5. recognize the importance of regulatory regimes for innovation and engineering design more generally;
6. apply principles of responsible innovation in engineering design.

# What is regulation?

* Broad definition of regulation
    * "any instrument (legal or non-legal, governmental or non-governmental) ... that is designed to channel behavior” (Brownsword and Somsen, 2009)
    * "intentional influencing of someone's or somethings behavior" (Black 2005)
* Clearly broader than 'law'
* The term governance is also sometimes used

# Forms of regulation

* European laws (adopted by European Council and European Parliament, e.g. GDPR);
* National legislation (e.g. The California Consumer Privacy Act);
* Executive orders/rules (e.g. Executive Order on Improving the Nation's Cybersecurity);
* Rules of regulatory agencies (e.g. FTC's Standards for Safeguarding Customer Information);
* Legal administrative decisions (e.g. decision of Dutch data protection authority/National competition authority).
* European and national case law (e.g. Schrems II case)

# Forms of regulation

# Property rights defined

* Cooter and Ulen: property is

"A bundle of legal rights over resources that the owner is free to exercise and whose exercise is protected from interference by others”

* Possessory rights and rights of transfer
* Property rights are not absolute
    * Governments can interfere when this is in the public interest and subject to the conditions provided for by law

# Economic rationale for property rights

1. Avoidance of dispute and of efforts to protect or to take things
2. Incentives to work (create, innovate etc.)
3. Incentives to maintain and improve things (innovate etc.)
4. Incentives to transfer things (ability to trade)

# Incentives to work (create, innovate etc.)

* If your output is not protected you have no incentives to work, or work more or less optimal from the point of view of social welfare (too little OR too much)

| Hours of work | Output | Utility from output | Disutility from work | Social welfare |

| 1 | 1 | 10 | 6 | 4 |

# Incentives to work (create, innovate etc.)

* Rights in one's output
    * the socially optimal outcome is achievable under property rights where each individual has rights in his own output;
    * if each individual has property rights in the output he produces, he will work the socially optimal amount and social welfare will be maximized
* Supervisor's rights in one's output
    * the socially optimal outcome is also achievable under property rights where a supervisory entity has rights in an individual's output if the supervisor can monitor individual's work

# Incentives to work

* Incentives and salary structure:
    * In real life (other than farm life...) incentives and salary structure established by supervisory entities where agents are employed
* Consequences of breakdown of property rights regime
    * During war there is no productive activity

# Incentives to maintain and improve things

* Improvements
    * When things may be taken from individuals, they will not benefit from improving them (if a machine would be taken from an individual, they will not have an incentive to maintain it well)
* Improvements and transfer rights
    * Socially optimal improvement in durable things will be promoted not only by possessory rights, but also by rights to transfer things.
    * Possessors will often transfer things by selling them and sales prices will generally reflect improvements made in things because present owners will anticipate this, they will be the case.

# Incentives to transfer things (ability to trade)

* Trade
    * the direct advantage of transfer of goods, that is, of trade, is that it raises the utilities of those who engage in it.
    * this may be because the preferences of individuals differ (hence by transfer we increase their well-being and utility)
* Specialization
    * Efficient methods of production take advantage of specialization and of agglomerations of individuals who devote themselves to making just one or several related goods (this argument goes back to Adam Smith)

# Intellectual property rights

* From an innovation policy standpoint, the concern about appropriation is particularly relevant because it could reduce incentives to invest in R&D
* R&D generates knowledge spillovers, which is good!
* But you don't want overinvestment, which would benefit only firms but not society as a whole
* IP rights are an (imperfect) mechanism to promote R&D investment at adequate levels

# How should property rights be allocated to achieve efficiency?

* But first what do we mean by efficiency?
* Efficient allocation
    * Pareto: makes everyone better-off
    * Kaldor-Hicks: increases the total value achieved by everyone in society even if it makes some people worse-off
* So how should property rights be allocated to achieve efficiency?
* Coase's surprising answer: **it doesn't matter**
* (Under certain conditions)

# The Coase Theorem

* Ronald Coase (1960), "The Problem of Social Cost"

* **In the absence of transaction costs, if property rights are well-defined and tradable, voluntary negotiations will lead to efficiency.**
    * It doesn't matter how rights are allocated initially...
    * ...because if they're allocated inefficiently at first, they can always be sold/traded...
    * so the allocation will end up efficient anyway

* Initial allocation does matter for **distribution**, though
    * And if there are **transaction costs**, may matter for **efficiency** too

# Externalities (or spillovers)

* General: Price does not accurately reflect social cost
    * Example: pollution
* Data-specific:
    * **Privacy externality**
        * users suffering privacy loss for which they are not compensated
    * **Information externality**
        * users do not internalize the cost of their information choices to other users
    * **Trust externality**
        * data breaches undermine confidence in overall data security and willingness to share data, thus affecting other data users and companies may not be charged for such breaches

# Information inadequacies

* Competitive markets can only function properly if consumers are sufficiently well informed to evaluate competing products or services
* But information production is costly
* There may be incentives not to disclose

# Unequal bargaining power

* Typically, due to problems with
    * competition
    * information
    * coordination
* Particularly when switching costs are high
* Acute problem in data markets - mainly data processors determine, within the legal framework, which personal data are collected and how they are used, rather than the party supplying data

## Conceptual framework for data policy

The role of data in the economy is large and growing. Due to external effects and unequal balance of power, the data economy is not functioning in an optimal way. Our framework—the pyramid on the right—provides insight into various types of solutions.

**Bottleneck: Unequal balance of power**
Data processors are often in a powerful position. Consumers and small businesses, therefore, have little or no choice and are in a poor bargaining position.

*Problem: data processors are too powerful*

**Solutions (Three types for a stronger data economy):**
1. Increase individual freedom of choice
2. Strengthen the collective
3. Direct government intervention

*Examples related to Large Differences in Bargaining Power (Top of Pyramid):*
* Example (3. Direct intervention): Mandatory data unbundling
* Example (2. Strengthen the collective): Collective data agreement

*Examples related to Negative Net External Affect (Bottom Left of Pyramid):*
* Problem: too many data are being shared
* Example (1. Individual choice): Restrictions on the collection of certain data
* Example (2. Collective): Standardized privacy agreements
* Example (3. Direct intervention): Public privacy databases

*Examples related to Positive Net External Effect (Bottom Right of Pyramid):*
* Problem: not enough data are shared
* Example (3. Direct intervention): Incentives for data sharing

**Bottleneck: external effects**
External effects arise because data sharing also impacts the privacy or user experience of others.

Source: Netherlands Bureau for Economic Policy Analysis (CPB Netherlands Bureau for Economic Policy Analysis, The Hague 2021)

# Public goods

* Individuals do not have the incentives to maintain and improve public goods or goods that
    * **non-excludable** individuals cannot be excluded from use or could be enjoyed without paying for it AND
    * **non-rivalrous** use by one individual does not reduce availability to others or the goods can be effectively consumed simultaneously by more than one person
* Consider a public meadow, any farmer can bring their cattle to the meadow, but end result could be that the meadow is destroyed
* Or consider clean air
* The tragedy of the commons
