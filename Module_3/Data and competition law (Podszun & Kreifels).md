## 1. INTRODUCTION

Three developments mark the advent of data as a topic in competition law.

In March 2016, the Bundeskartellamt, the German competition author-ity, published the news that it had initiated proceedings against Facebook.¹ The authority ran a case against the company for abuse of a dominant position by data protection infringements. This was the first enforcement action directly focussing on data in competition law. Before, other cases had dealt with data as assets, but the relationship of data and competition had not come into such focus before. Following the pioneering initiation of proceedings the Bundeskartellamt and the Autorité de la concurrence, the French competition authority, published a paper on **Competition Law and Data**².

In August 2016, US scholars Maurice Stucke and Allan Grunes published their book **Big Data and Competition Policy**.³ The book soon became a reference point for the academic debate on the topic that picked up. Data and competition had been around as a topic already, of course,

### 1.2 Why Data Is a Topic for Competition Law

The Microsoft case highlights one situation in which data – here: the refusal to disclose data – may influence competition in markets. With the rise of data as a most valuable resource it was necessary for competition authorities and courts to take this aspect of making business into account. Competition law provides for the most basic rules that make markets function. In order to apply the rules of competition law it is necessary to understand markets, business models and the parameters that decide about success and failure in competition. This stocktaking of the economic environment in a specific case is usually labelled market definition.¹⁶ If new forces start to influence markets, such forces need to be taken into account. With the rise in importance of data for success in markets, data became a critical feature of competition law analysis.

As such, data as an asset is not different from other assets of companies, e.g., a production plant or the workforce. Data differs from other assets, however, in that the information contained is not rivalrous, which means that data can be used by different companies in parallel (other than tangi-ble goods). The aim of competition law is to keep markets open and to protect the process of competition as the underlying mechanism of the market economy. Competition aims at preventing market failures, thereby guar-anteeing three effects: an efficient allocation of resources in society; a need for companies to become ever more productive and innovative; and the preservation of the freedom of market participants to decide autono-mously according to their individual preferences.¹⁸

Data may have a positive, pro-competitive impact in this endeavour: the more information is available and the better such information can be processed, the better the matching of markets. Information asymmetries, intransparencies and misallocations due to wrong or misleading informa-tion may be reduced.¹⁹ So, a data-driven economy has the potential to push efficiency, innovation and consumer welfare by enabling better coordina-tion processes in the market.²⁰

Competition law regimes usually rest on three pillars to avoid failures in these market processes. First, it is prohibited to enter into agreements that restrict competition. Companies may not reduce the typical risks of doing business in competing with others by coordinating their behaviour. This prohibition of coordinated business practices is laid down in European law in Art. 101 TFEU. In particular, the collusion of competitors is prohibited ('cartel'), yet restrictive business practices may also be found in vertical relationships, e.g., in distribution contracts between producers and retailers, or in licensing agreements. Competitors may not even share information without running the risk of being fined. Sharing sensitive business data may be a problem under competition law, but it may also be problematic when companies enter into contracts that exclude others from using certain data.

### 1.3 Working with Data in Competition Matters

When discussing the significance of data in competition law it should be borne in mind that the enforcement of competition law itself is largely dependent on data. Arguably, competition law is the most data-prone field of the law. This is rooted in the close ties of this field of law with econom-ics. Competition law serves the purpose of making the economy work properly, and many core concepts – such as 'restriction of competition' (Art. 101(1) TFEU), 'improving the production or distribution of goods' (Art. 101(3) TFEU), 'significantly impede effective competition' (Art. 2(2) EC Merger Regulation), 'abuse' (Art. 102 TFEU) – are loaded with eco-nomic background.

## 2. CHALLENGES FOR COMPETITION LAW CONCEPTS

Are the rules of competition law still fit to integrate the latest develop-ments of the digital economy? Or is there a need for reform?

Since the terminology in the norms is rather vague (e.g., 'abuse'), competition law agencies and courts are generally free to deal with new economic phenomena by interpretation of the norms. Such an approach, however, has three limitations. First, a rigid re-interpretation of the word-ing conflicts with the separation of powers between legislature and the other branches. Secondly, in practice, such a re-interpretation takes a lot of time since courts and agencies try to avoid disruptive elements in their reading of the norms. The case-law may evolve over time with the changes but quick and fundamental changes should be introduced not on the arbitrariness of cases coming up but by a legislative activity. Finally, it may be the case that markets in the digital economy are structurally changing the whole way of doing business. If the working mechanisms of the digital economy fundamentally differ from those of the industrial economy, a re-interpretation of the wording may not suffice.

In this subsection, we identify the paradigm shifts associated with the data economy.²⁸ Whether these changes are fundamental and whether they prompt a shift in policy are still disputed.

### 2.1 Quantification Issues

Competition law, particularly in the age of an effects-based approach, relies heavily on exact information that can be quantified. This quantifica-tion is usually done by taking financial proxies. To give four examples.

The thresholds for merger control relate to the turnover of a company (e.g., in Art. 1(2) EC Merger Control Regulation). What if a company does not have a relevant financial turnover but has assembled zillions of pieces of most interesting data? Does the concentration go unchecked for failure to reach the turnover thresholds?

An important tool for market definition is the SSNIP-test. The SSNIP-test asks whether the demand for one specific product would shift to another product in case of a small, but significant, non-transitory increase in price.²⁹

Business models involving data are often coupled with free services. Most notably, users of Facebook or Uber partly 'pay' for services with their data. While contract lawyers may struggle with the question whether this payment amounts to consideration,³⁰ competition lawyers need to accom-modate these market transactions in their patterns of analysis. Since this analysis is largely effects-based and requires concrete figures, the analysis of data-driven business models and markets will remain on shaky grounds.

Platforms operating on multi-sided markets can become powerful. ### 2.4 Algorithms and AI

The first field for the successful application of big data was targeted adver-tising by media companies.⁴⁰ Knowing the characteristics and preferences of customers enables companies to target their customers much better.

While such use of customer data does not change the economy in a paradigmatic way, the use of data as input for feeding algorithms and artificial intelligence has the potential to change competition as such. Algorithms, i.e., clear procedural instructions to solve a problem, are more and more in use for determining decisions. It is no longer individuals that meet for coordination in the marketplace but algorithms fed with certain data that decide upon supply, demand and price. Competition law rules have as their underlying idea (as most legal norms) autonomous decisions by individuals who are responsible for their acts. This may change.

The more intelligent machines become (with machine-learning and artificial intelligence) the more difficult it will be to regulate the most basic principles of the market. Just to give one example: if the pricing of goods by retailers is done by machines that are fed with the results of price crawlers (identifying the prices in the market) and that are programmed for profit, such machines may find that an alignment of prices (formerly known as a cartel) may be the best way of price-setting in the market. Dependent upon the intelligence of the machine such a cartel may even be covered up in a way that it can hardly be detected by the outside world.⁴¹

Such scenarios of dimming competitive pressure through machines show the potential threats to competition and shed some light on the deficits of traditional competition law enforcement.

### 2.5 Dynamics and Disruption

A final problem that should be highlighted in relation to the digital economy is a conceptual disagreement whether competition is 'just one click away' or whether market structures have been stabilised to a degree without any real competition. In the past, companies like Yahoo, Myspace, Nokia or Netscape seemed invincible, but now they are insig-nificant. Yet, Google, Amazon, Facebook, Apple and Microsoft (often referred to as GAFAM) have been solidly successful for more than ten years.

Do regulators that try to protect competition aim at a moving target? Have markets become so dynamic that they regulate themselves without need for governmental intervention? Is it still possible that disruptive innovations or innovative start-ups will reduce the GAFAM companies to the status of Yahoo with just one click?

On the latter point, it seems noteworthy that the very successful Internet companies have organised means of protecting their market positions. The first is their wealth in data that makes it hard to know better than these companies what customers are willing to pay for. Secondly, the enormous financial means accumulated in the past years enable them to buy off or copy competitors that may endanger their business models.

The dynamics of the digital economy can hardly be measured with the traditional tools of competition law. Not only do they focus on financial means (see above), but also the analysis is often a static snapshot analysis. The toolbox of competition does not allow for taking due account of market dynamics and the evolution of markets.⁴³ This is a conceptual deficit that is hard-felt in the digital economy.

## 3. MERGER CONTROL

In cases of concentration merger control is a pre-emptive tool that identi-fies a significant impediment of effective competition (SIEC) at an early stage, namely before the merger is implemented. Mergers can be categorised as horizontal, vertical or conglomerate with each of the groups of mergers having different effects for competition. Horizontal mergers, i.e., mergers of competitors on the same market level, lead to the elimination of the competi-tive constraint by a market player (enabling non-coordinated effects) and the easier collusion of remaining competitors (coordinated effects).⁴⁴ Vertical mergers may lead to a foreclosure of possibilities for supply or demand. Conglomerate mergers involve companies whose relationship is neither hori-zontal nor vertical.⁴⁵ They have been rarely challenged in the past.⁴⁶

Horizontal and vertical data-driven mergers pose challenges to competi-tion authorities since it is so difficult to assess the value of data for market power or market foreclosure. It becomes obvious that the competitive analysis of these cases has gaps, particularly with regard to the assessment of data markets where data itself is traded (and not just seen as an input for other goods or services). Even though the Commission takes into account the impact of data on specific markets it hardly devotes resources to a thorough investigation in data markets.

Conglomerate mergers that might have anticompetitive effects usually involve companies that are active in closely related markets.⁴⁷ The classic example is that of the **Tetra Laval/Sidel** case, a merger challenged by the European Commission, where a producer of carton packaging acquired a producer of PET packaging technology.⁴⁸ The classic theory of harm in such cases runs along the lines of leveraging effects where one party may use its dominance on one market to conquer – by power, not performance – a neighbouring market. Yet, many conglomerate data-driven mergers do not involve neighbouring markets. Therefore competition authorities lack existing guidelines to assess these types of mergers.⁴⁹

## 4. ABUSE OF MARKET POWER

Cases dealing with the abuse of market power are rare in competition law, but they are particularly significant since they challenge the most power-ful undertakings and need to define what constitutes an 'abuse'. This is a difficult term since abuse is an antonym to the proper use of competitive parameters. Yet, the 'discovery procedure' of competition (Friedrich A. von Hayek) lives upon the unexpected use, the surprising moves and non-conformist market strategies. Abuse cases involving new phenomena, such as big data, run the risk of having chilling effects for competition.

### 4.1 Google-Shopping

Since the rise of the digital economy Google has been a popular target of the European competition watchdog. Consequently, the main role of the most famous case concerning the digital economy thus far was given to Google for abusing a dominant position in the general Internet search markets. Not only did it take seven years until the Commission took a final decision; at 2.42 billion euros, the fine has been the highest ever imposed on a single undertaking thus far. In other words, Google would leverage its competitive strength in the search market to increase its strength in the market for shopping comparison sites. In almost all EEA countries Google has been dominant in the general Internet search markets since 2008, exceeding market shares of 90 per cent in most countries.⁹² The preferential treatment of its own service and demotion of competitors' services would substantially strengthen Google Shopping's visibility and make alternative services virtually invisible. ## 5. RESTRICTIVE BUSINESS PRACTICES

It appears to be inevitable that technological advances and big data have a significant impact on possible anticompetitive agreements or concerted practices.¹⁰³ There has not been much empirical evidence yet. However, in some cases courts and competition authorities have already had to adapt to these changes.

### 5.1 Amazon Poster Case

The US Department of Justice (as the acting antitrust authority) dealt with undertakings that engaged in the sale of posters through **Amazon Marketplace** in the US. The cartel members agreed to fix, increase, main-tain and stabilise prices. To implement these agreements the conspirators adopted specific pricing algorithms to coordinate changes to their respec-tive prices.¹⁰⁴ In this case competition law was infringed by a common price-fixing agreement that was implemented through data-fed algorithms. The main feature of this case is that the respective algorithms served to implement these agreements, thus significantly facilitating anticompetitive conduct by technical means.

As pointed out earlier, such a constellation may even arise with machine-learning without the consent of individuals to collusion.

### 5.3 Data Pooling (Asnef Equifax)

Apart from these cases where data and digitalisation are particularly rel-evant for the implementation side of the antitrust infringement, there are more classic and mainstream cases involving restrictive business practices in the field of data. The leading decision is the **Asnef Equifax** decision by the European Court of Justice.

This ruling deals with data sharing. The case, decided in 2006 prior to discussions about big data, concerns a register of information between financial institutions on the solvency of customers.¹¹³ The Court noted that such information systems generally do not constitute an infringement under competition law. However, the legality of such data pooling or sharing is highly dependent on the economic and legal context in which the register exists. First, if supply on a market is highly concentrated, the exchange of specific data may constitute an infringement. Secondly, it is necessary that the system does not allow lenders to be identified (aggregated and anonymised data). Thirdly, the conditions of access and use by financial institutions may not be discriminatory.¹¹⁴ So, ‘club solu-tions', e.g., excluding some players, were not allowed. Additionally, the ECJ highlighted the possible beneficial nature of the effect on consumers in the relevant markets. Such registers could be capable of preventing over-indebtedness for consumers of credit. Even if the conduct in question restricted competition these objective economic advantages might offset the disadvantages of possible restriction.¹¹⁵

*Asnef Equifax* is the leading case for sharing, pooling or exchanging data, and it informed the Commission's communication on informa-tion exchange in the Horizontal Guidelines.¹¹⁶ Information sharing may amount to a hard-core restriction of competition, if the data exchange among competitors concerns current, individualised, concrete data with relevance for business that is exchanged frequently. The conditions set out in the case-law for an unproblematic data exchange are rigid: in particular, it is impossible to gain a competitive advantage with only some market participants instead of all. Information sharing has traditionally been in the focus of competition authorities.¹²¹ Yet, the law is based on cases that played in the 'old world', not the one with the high-speed exchange of enormous masses of data of today. ## 6. REGULATORY RESPONSES AND RELATED QUESTIONS

As shown data issues challenge the concepts of competition law because traditional working mechanisms of markets have been transformed in several sectors. The rise of platforms and the use of data have a fundamen-tal impact on the working mechanisms of competition. The development continues with artificial intelligence and an increasing use of algorithms.

All three pillars the competition law regime rests upon are affected by this paradigm shift. The merger cases we presented shed light on the difficulty of dealing with such data-driven mergers. So far data markets have not even been analysed thoroughly by the competition authorities. Concerning potential abuse of dominance different theories of harm are forged. With regard to restrictive business initial signs can be seen that there are changes to the implementation of agreements or concerted practices. These changes call for regulatory responses.

The German legislator was the first to take legislative steps in response to the digital changes with the ninth amendment of the Act against Restraints of Competition (ARC) in 2017.¹²⁴ Three aspects deserve men-tion. First, the merger control thresholds were amended. According to § 35 (1a) of the ARC, a merger needs to be notified and cleared even if the turnover thresholds are not reached if the value of the transaction exceeds 400 million euros. This is a reaction to the failure of the ARC to catch the Facebook/WhatsApp merger. Secondly, it was clarified in § 18 (2a) that markets without financial consideration may still constitute markets in the sense of the law. Thus, markets with gratuitous services are – without a doubt – part of the economy that is subject to the rules of competition. Thirdly, the criteria for assessing market power were updated in § 18 (3a) ARC. The new norm mentions as factors for market power network effects: multi-homing and switching costs, economies of scale related to network effects, access to relevant data, and innovation-driven competitive pressure. These criteria highlight that the economy is undergoing change – even if these rules do not substantially alter competition law.

While there are many initiatives regarding the digital economy in differ-ent Directorates General of the European Commission, DG Competition has not yet put forward more ambitious policy plans.
