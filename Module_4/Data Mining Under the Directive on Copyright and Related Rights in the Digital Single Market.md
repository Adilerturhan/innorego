# Data Mining Under the Directive on Copyright and Related Rights in the Digital Single Market: Are European Database Protection Rules Still Threatening the Development of Artificial Intelligence?

> This paper explores how the existing European rules on the legal and contractual protection of databases limit the re-use of non-personal data by start-ups and SMEs for the purpose of developing artificial intelligence in the European Union. This analysis aims to determine whether the recent initiatives on data mining and data sharing are adequate to ensure an appropriate level of data re-usability for that purpose. In turn, this paper argues that additional reforms are needed to establish a more balanced European framework on the legal and contractual protection of databases. Therefore, it contemplates the introduction of data user rights, which would facilitate the access and re-use of non-personal data by the enterprises in question.

## I. Introduction

Over the last five years, a number of legal controversies and political debates have blossomed concerning how to properly apprehend data in terms of mobility, access and re-use in the process of building a data economy for Europe.¹

To this day, there is no consensus on a uniform defini-tion of 'data'. Due to their all-encompassing nature,³ data can take many forms, including words, images, sounds, videos, numbers, maps and charts. They can be aggregated in various forms of datasets, either structured or unstructured, which can be openly accessible or re-stricted to a few users.⁵ In recent years, scholars have emphasised the value which can stem from the processing of such datasets through innovative tools that enable insights and discoveries such as artificial intelligence.⁶

Despite the great amount of literature on the topic, there is no consensus on a uniform definition of 'artificial intelligence' (hereinafter ‘AI’) either.⁷ In practical terms, Al is an algorithm-based technology that needs tremen-dous quantities of data to be properly trained.⁸ More spe-cifically, the development of AI relies on machine learning techniques, which provide the algorithm with the ability to improve its performances through experience.⁹

This form of self-learning relies notably on the method of data mining, understood as the automatic or semi-automatic process by which large quantities of data are manipulated in order to uncover new patterns.¹⁰ It involves different steps, which may be presented as follows:

i. preparation, which usually involves identifying the relevant data from one or several sources, followed by copying and loading the data into a dataset, where it is sometimes pre-processed or transformed;
ii. extraction, which refers to running the algorithm on the dataset in order to identify and evaluate correla-tions between the data; and
iii. recombination, which refers to the rearrangement of the data in order to obtain and visualise the results of the processing.¹¹

From the outset, it seems that the focus has been on EU copyright rules and how they potentially limit the devel-opment of AI, whereas surprisingly little attention has been paid to EU database protection rules in that re-gard.¹⁵ Accordingly, the present paper focuses on how the existing EU database protection rules potentially limit the development of AI in order to assess whether these new rules are adequate to ensure an appropriate level of data re-usability for innovative start-ups and small and medium-sized enterprises (hereinafter 'SMEs')¹⁶ that want to develop new forms of AI in the European Union.

Due to the focus placed by the European Commission on making more data available, this paper only covers datasets that have been made available with the consent of the rightholder.¹⁷ Furthermore, these datasets will only be looked at from the perspective of intellectual property and contracts.¹⁸ Among existing forms of intellectual property rights, this paper is further limited to the protec-tion of databases by copyright and the sui generis right.¹⁹

As a necessary first step, the existing EU framework on database protection will be reviewed to identify the cur-rent limitations on data re-usability that burden the devel-opment of AI by start-ups and SMEs in the European Union (Part II). Based on the limitations identified, the new rules on data mining and data sharing will be assessed to determine whether they can facilitate the development of Al by start-ups and SMEs in the European Union (Part III). Based on these findings, rec-ommendations will be put forward where appropriate to improve the existing framework on the matter of data re-usability (Part IV).

## II. The existing limitations on data re-usability: database protection in the EU

As a first step, attention must be paid to the existing limi-tations on data re-usability in the context of the develop-ment of AI, which may arise (1) from the legal protection of databases and (2) from the contractual protection of databases.

### 1. The legal limitations on data re-usability

In principle, data are not owned by anyone and can there-fore be freely re-used by everyone.²⁰ That being said, data re-usability may be limited in the EU on the basis of the Directive on the legal protection of databases (hereinafter ‘Database Directive’).²¹

**a) The notion of 'database'**

The Database Directive defines a database as 'a collection of independent works, data or other materials arranged in a systematic or methodical way and individually acces-sible by electronic or other means'.²²

Firstly, the term 'collection' implies that several ele-ments must be gathered in order to form a coherent whole.²³

Secondly, these elements must be 'independent' from one another in the sense that each element must retain an autonomous informative value even after its extraction from the collection.²⁴

Thirdly, the collection may generally encompass 'works, data and other materials'. As the notion of 'other materials' is not defined, there is considerable room for elements of various forms to fall within the scope of the Directive.²⁵

Fourthly, these elements must be arranged in a 'system-atic or methodical way', which implies that the collection cannot be organised in a way that is arbitrary, accidental or hazardous.²⁶

Finally, it is necessary that the elements are 'individu-ally accessible' in the sense that it must be possible to re-trieve any of them within the collection by way of 'electronic' means (such as a search engine) or 'other means' (such as an index, a plan or a table of contents).²⁷

The application of these criteria has shown that they leave room for various instances, such as websites, cata-logues, address lists, schedules, telephone directories, grids of pharmaceutical results and calendars.²⁸ Therefore, the threshold for a dataset to qualify as a data-base is arguably low.²⁹ However, it does not suffice to trigger the legal protection provided by the Database Directive,³⁰ as will be further demonstrated below.

**b) The copyright and sui generis protection of databases**

Provided that it qualifies as a 'database', a dataset may benefit from copyright protection, *sui generis* protection or even the combination of both if the relevant conditions are fulfilled.³¹ Importantly, the two forms of protection differ in scope: copyright protection extends to the struc-ture of the database rather than its contents,³² whereas *sui generis* protection extends to the contents of the data-base considered in whole or in substantial parts,³³ as will be further explained below.

**(a) The copyright protection of databases**

The copyright on a database is held by its author, defined as the natural person or group of natural persons who created the database.³⁴ The copyright protection is trig-gered by the fact that the database consists in the author's 'own intellectual creation' by reason of the selection or ar-rangement of its contents, regardless of any other criteria.³⁵

In that regard, the CJEU held that the structure of a database can be protected by copyright if it is 'original'— namely, when the selection or arrangement of its contents results from the making of free and creative choices on the part of the author, by which he leaves his personal stamp—whereas such protection does not arise if the structure of the database is dictated by 'technical consid-erations, rules or constraints'.³⁶

In practice, digital databases tend to be comprehensive rather than selective and therefore often lack the neces-sary originality in their arrangement.³⁷ In addition, most database structures follow a logical, alphabetical or chro-nological order which is deprived of originality in order to facilitate the use of the database.³⁸ Based on the above considerations, many databases are thus effectively de-prived of copyright protection.³⁹

Be that as it may, when a database does fall within its scope, the copyright protection on its structure lasts for 70 years from the first of January of the year following the death of its author.⁴⁰

Until then, the author (and, after his death, his descend-ants) is entitled to prevent third parties from accomplish-ing two types of unauthorised acts.⁴¹ On the one hand, the author has an exclusive right over the reproduction of the original structure of the database, which will be in-fringed every time it is copied from one medium to an-other without his prior authorisation.⁴² On the other hand, the author has an exclusive right over the commu-nication to the public of the original structure of the data-base, which will be infringed whenever it is made available to an indefinite amount of potential recipients without his prior authorisation.⁴³

As indicated above, the development of Al requires complete datasets to ensure the accuracy of its results. When at least one of these datasets qualifies as a database protected by copyright, unauthorised activities may take place throughout the data mining process:

i. At the stage of preparation, the database might be copied in its entirety, including its original structure, and uploaded into another dataset to be mined, which would amount to an unauthorised reproduction.⁴⁴ Furthermore, the protected aspects of the database might have to be pre-processed in order to give it a format that would facilitate the subsequent mining, which would also amount to an unauthorised repro-duction.⁴⁵ However, no unauthorised communication to the public would take place, as the database would not be made available to a new public but only pre-pared for further processing.⁴⁶

ii. At the stage of extraction, the running of the algo-rithm on the dataset would constitute an infringement on the right of reproduction if one or several copies of the original structure of the database were made in the process.⁴⁷ In any case, no unauthorised communi-cation to the public would take place as the database would not be made available to a new public but only processed through the algorithm.⁴⁸

iii. At the stage of recombination, it is unlikely that any unauthorised reproduction or communication to the public would take place as it is usually impossible to identify the original structure of the database in the fi-nal output.⁴⁹

To conclude, the development of AI may involve copy-right infringing activities when it consists in the mining of at least one database protected by copyright as the pro-cess might involve several unauthorised reproductions of its original structure.

**(b) The sui generis protection of databases**

The *sui generis* right on a database is held by its maker, defined as the person who took the initiative and the risk of investing.⁵⁰ *Sui generis* protection is triggered by the fact that a substantial investment, measured quantitatively and/or qualitatively, was re-quired in obtaining, verifying or presenting the data-base contents.⁵¹

The 'investment' required can be financial (e.g. spend-ing money), material (e.g. acquiring equipment) or human (e.g. spending time, energy and effort).⁵² The Directive does not further define the threshold for such an invest-ment to qualify as 'substantial' and, to this day, the CJEU has not provided any guidance in that regard.⁵³ Therefore, this issue is left to the interpretation of na-tional courts, although the level of investment required should nevertheless be quite low.⁵⁴

Be that as it may, the CJEU did provide some guid-ance on the activities in which that investment is required.⁵⁵

Firstly, the act of 'obtaining' refers to the gathering of existing materials to be included in the database.⁵⁶ On that point, an important distinction must be drawn be-tween the investments made in the collection of the mate-rials (which, if substantial, may trigger the application of *sui generis* protection) and the creation of the materials (which, however substantial, may never trigger the appli-cation of *sui generis* protection).⁵⁷

Secondly, 'verification' refers to the monitoring of the accuracy of the gathered materials in order to ensure the reliability of the contents of the database, be it at the time of its creation or during its operation.⁵⁸ In practice, checking, correcting and updating the contents of the database are ways to ensure their reliability and therefore qualify as verification.⁵⁹

Finally, 'presentation' refers to the organisation of the gathered materials, which includes the structuration of the database as well as the implementation of electronic means in order to ensure that the elements it contains are individually accessible.⁶⁰

When these conditions are fulfilled, the *sui generis* pro-tection lasts for 15 years from the first of January follow-ing the date of the database's completion.⁶¹ However, this term can be renewed with every substantial change made to the contents of the protected database.⁶² As a re-sult, a database whose contents are regularly updated will enjoy permanent protection.⁶³

As long as the *sui generis* protection lasts, the database maker is entitled to prevent third parties from accom-plishing two types of unauthorised acts.⁶⁴ On the one hand, the database maker has an exclusive right over the extraction of the database contents, which will be in-fringed every time a substantial part of the protected aspects of the database is transferred from the database to another medium without the prior authorisation of the database maker.⁶⁵ On the other hand, the database maker has an exclusive right over the re-utilisation of the database contents, which will be infringed every time a substantial part of the protected aspects of the database is made available to the public without the prior authorisa-tion of the database maker.⁶⁶

What the database maker cannot do, however, is pre-vent third parties from extracting or re-utilising insub-stantial parts of the contents of a database—that is, of course, unless such acts are accomplished repeatedly in order to reconstruct a substantial part of the database.⁶⁷

In particular, the Database Directive bans the repeated and systematic extraction of insubstantial parts of the contents of the database when such acts are in conflict with the normal exploitation of the database or when they unreasonably prejudice the legitimate interests of the database maker.⁶⁸ Applied to the field of AI, this specific provision brings the following question to mind: does the mining of a protected database by an algorithm (such as the mining of a website or any other form of electronic database) qualify as a repeated and systematic extraction of insubstantial parts of that database, which can ulti-mately be forbidden by the database maker?

As indicated above, the development of AI requires complete datasets in order to ensure the accuracy of its results. When at least one of these datasets qualifies as a database covered by the *sui generis* right, unauthorised activities may take place throughout the data mining process:

i. At the stage of preparation, it is likely that a substan-tial part (if not the entirety) of the protected aspects of the database will be transferred from their source to the dataset where they will be mined, which amounts to an unauthorised extraction.⁶⁹ However, no unauthorised re-utilisation would take place as the database would not be made available to a new public.⁷⁰

ii. At the stage of extraction, running the algorithm on the dataset would constitute an infringement on the right of extraction if it involved the transfer of a sub-stantial part of the protected aspects of the database within the algorithm, which is likely to occur in most cases.⁷¹ Conversely, no unauthorised re-utilisation would take place as the protected aspects of the data-base would not be made available to a new public.⁷²

iii. At the stage of recombination, it is unlikely that a sub-stantial part of the protected aspects of the database would be transferred as a result of the mining as the output merely reports on the results of the process and does not typically include any extracts that can be linked to the mined materials, therefore excluding any infringement on the right of extraction and re-utilisation.⁷³

To conclude, as was the case for copyright, the devel-opment of AI may involve activities infringing the *sui ge-neris* right. A repeated and systematic extraction of insubstantial parts is indeed likely to occur throughout the data mining process.⁷⁴ Taken as a whole, these insub-stantial extractions allow the algorithm to thoroughly an-alyse the contents of the database such that a substantial part of the protected database is gradually processed by the algorithm in the course of its mining activities.⁷⁵ As such activities seem comparable to a substantial extrac-tion, they may be subject to the prior authorisation of the database maker in order to safeguard his investments in obtaining, verifying and presenting the protected aspects of the database.⁷⁶

**(c) The exceptions to the copyright and sui generis protection of databases**

After finding that copyright and the *sui generis* right con-stitute an obstacle to data re-usability, it must be evalu-ated whether start-ups and SMEs may nevertheless benefit from the exceptions to the legal protection of databases such that they can freely re-use their contents for the purpose of developing AI.

Exceptions to copyright and the *sui generis* right typi-cally authorise certain uses of the protected aspects of a database without the consent of the relevant rightholders as such uses are deemed to serve an important public interest.⁷⁷

**(a) The optional exceptions**

The Database Directive gives Member States the possibil-ity to provide for several optional limitations on the copy-right and *sui generis* protection of databases.⁷⁸

Firstly, Member States may provide for a limitation in the case of a reproduction or an extraction of a non-electronic database for private purposes.⁷⁹ This exception does not apply to databases in electronic format for com-mercial purposes and is therefore irrelevant for start-ups and SMEs that want to develop new forms of AI.⁸⁰

Secondly, Member States may provide for a limitation in the case of a reproduction or an extraction in the con-text of scientific research, provided that the source is indi-cated and the purpose is non-commercial.⁸¹ The notion of 'scientific research' refers to the natural and human scien-ces,⁸² which begs the question of its application to the de-velopment of AI.⁸³ In any case, the indication of the source is impractical in the process of training AI, and the limitation on non-commercial purposes rules out start-ups and SMEs from the scope of the exception.⁸⁴

Thirdly, Member States may provide for a limitation for the purposes of public security or an administrative or judicial procedure,⁸⁵ which hardly refers to the potential use of data mining techniques by start-ups and SMEs in the context of the development of AI.

To conclude, none of the optional exceptions seem rele-vant regarding the unauthorised reproductions and extractions made by start-ups and SMEs during the train-ing of an Al system.

**(b) The mandatory exceptions**

The Database Directive ensures that lawful users can ac-complish certain acts prohibited by copyright and the *sui generis* right.⁸⁶ Although the notion of 'lawful user' is not defined, the CJEU has found that it extends to anyone 'whose access to the contents of a database for the pur-pose of consultation results from the direct or indirect consent' of the rightholder.⁸⁷

In the digital environment, the notions of 'lawful user' and 'lawful access' are linked, as the CJEU has gradually recognised that lawful access to a source is a precondition for the lawful use of that source.⁸⁸

To determine whether the source was lawfully accessed, the first step is to look at the rightholder's con-sent: if the source was made available to the public with the rightholder's consent, the access is deemed to be law-ful, whereas if the source was made available to the public without the rightholder's consent, the access is deemed to be unlawful.⁸⁹

When the access is deemed to be unlawful, the second step is to determine whether the user knew, or should have known, that the source was made available without the rightholder's consent, in which case the use is deemed unlawful.⁹⁰ Importantly, this knowledge will be presumed in the context of a profit-making activity.⁹¹

In practice, these distinctions are of course difficult to make and heavily depend on the circumstances of the case. It seems reasonable to consider that a database made available online with the consent of the database maker can be lawfully used by everyone,⁹² whereas a database made available without the consent of the data-base maker can only be lawfully used by non-commercial end-users acting in good faith.⁹³ As start-ups and SMEs are profit-oriented, they will be confronted with the pre-sumption that they are acting in bad faith in cases where they conduct data mining activities on openly accessible websites whose contents were made available without the rightholder's consent.

Part of the literature considers that the notion of 'law-ful user' is not limited to users who obtained the consent of the rightholder⁹⁴ but also extends to users benefiting from one of the exceptions introduced in the Database Directive.⁹⁵ In any case, in the absence of any applicable exception, it seems that start-ups and SMEs will always need to obtain some form of consent from the rightholder to qualify as lawful users, be it explicitly (on the basis of a contract) or implicitly (on the basis of the making a data-base available without restrictions).⁹⁶

With regard to databases protected by copyright, the Database Directive ensures that lawful users can use the database contents normally.⁹⁷ It has been argued that the condition of 'normal use' is redundant as it would merely refer to the fact that the use must be in accordance with the contractual authorisation or the legal exception which it is based on.⁹⁸ Since start-ups and SMEs do not adequately benefit from any exception, it follows that the mining of the database would have to be conducted in ac-cordance with the authorisation given by the rightholder.

If the rightholder made his protected database available without any specification, it could be argued that start-ups and SMEs would be entitled to re-use it in the context of data mining activities. Conversely, such activities could not be conducted if the rightholder made the database available under strict conditions that limit the possibility to mine the database contents without its consent.

With regard to databases protected by the *sui generis* right, the Database Directive ensures that the lawful user has the right to extract and re-utilise insubstantial parts of the contents of a database made available to the public for any purpose whatsoever.⁹⁹ Since start-ups and SMEs generally need complete datasets from which substantial parts are extracted in the context of their data mining ac-tivities, the limitation to 'insubstantial parts' renders this exception useless for the purpose of developing AI.¹⁰⁰

To conclude, none of the exceptions introduced by the Database Directive provides sufficient legal certainty con-cerning the possibility for start-ups and SMEs to re-use protected databases for the purpose of developing AI. Therefore, the copyright and *sui generis* protection of databases undeniably constitutes an obstacle to data re-usability.¹⁰¹

### 2. The contractual limitations on data re-usability

In the digital environment, data holders often rely on con-tractual provisions to control the re-use of their data-bases.¹⁰² In turn, data users are often confronted with the 'rent-seeking behaviour' of data holders, whose contractual terms reduce the possibility of data re-use.¹⁰³ Therefore, it is important to assess the existing limitations on data re-usability which stem from the contractual protection of databases and their impact on start-ups and SMEs.

**a) The contractual protection of protected databases**

The Database Directive is without prejudice to the law of contract, which gives rightholders the possibility to ex-tend by contract the protection granted by law to their databases.¹⁰⁴ In practice, rightholders do not rely on con-tracts as a replacement for the legal protection granted to their databases but rather as a useful complement to that protection.¹⁰⁵

As explained in the previous part, the Database Directive restricts that possibility by providing that con-tracts cannot limit the rights of the lawful user.¹⁰⁶ Conversely, the Database Directive does not contain a similar provision concerning the optional exceptions to the copyright and *sui generis* protection, which righthold-ers can therefore override by contract.¹⁰⁷

For one thing, contractual terms which exclude the appli-cation of the optional exceptions should have little impact on start-ups and SMEs as these exceptions are in any case not applicable to them.¹⁰⁸ That being said, contractual terms which limit the re-usability of a protected database beyond the scope of the copyright and *sui generis* protection may be problematic in the sense that start-ups and SMEs might be confronted with highly restrictive and/or poorly drafted clauses which make the will of rightholders hard to decipher and the re-usability of data uncertain.¹⁰⁹

To conclude, contracts have the potential to further re-strict the possibility of re-using data when they serve as a complement to the legal protection of databases, which is likely to have a negative impact on the development of AI by start-ups and SMEs.

**b) The contractual protection of unprotected databases**

In principle, databases which are in the public domain may be freely re-used.¹¹⁰ By default, all databases are in the public domain unless they are protected by an intellec-tual property right.¹¹¹ In that regard, the question arises whether data holders can rely on contracts to limit the re-use of databases which are not subject to any intellectual property right.¹¹²

This is precisely the question which the CJEU had to answer in a case between *Ryanair* and *PR Aviation*.¹¹³ The question asked to the Court was, in essence, whether *Ryanair* could rely on the general terms and conditions applicable to its website, which qualified as a database but was neither eligible for copyright nor for the *sui ge-neris* right, in order to prevent *PR Aviation* from re-using the data it contained for commercial purposes.¹¹⁴ On ref-erence, the CJEU answered that the Database Directive does not prevent the laying down of contractual limita-tions on the use of a database ineligible for copyright and *sui generis* protection.¹¹⁵

The implications of this ruling are profound as it enti-tles data holders to determine the scope of the contractual protection of their unprotected databases without consid-eration for any type of user.¹¹⁶ In other terms, it acknowl-edges the right of data holders to use contracts to retrieve databases from the public domain and to give them more protection than would otherwise have been permitted by law, which amounts to no less than 'private legislation'.¹¹⁷

Awarding a potentially stronger level of protection (by contract) to unprotected databases (by law) threatens to deprive the intellectual property system of its meaning.¹¹⁸ Furthermore, as the contractual protection of otherwise unprotected databases is not harmonised, its enforcement is likely to vary from one Member State to the other. As a result, the data holder prerogatives are likely to be defined differently across the European Union, which has the po-tential to endanger the proper functioning of the Digital Single Market.¹¹⁹

Based on the above considerations, contracts used to retrieve unprotected databases from the public domain have a negative impact on start-ups and SMEs since they entail the possibility of precluding the re-use of their con-tents for the purpose of developing AI.

**c) Conclusion**

Several conclusions may be drawn from the existing limi-tations on data re-usability encountered by start-ups and SMEs in the context of the development of AI.

Firstly, the copyright protection of databases consti-tutes an obstacle to data re-usability for the purpose of developing AI as no reproduction may occur without the prior authorisation of the author and no exception may clearly benefit start-ups and SMEs in that regard. However, considering that the 'originality' standard is not easy to fulfil, a significant amount of databases are left without copyright protection, which reduces the im-pact of the copyright protection on data re-usability.

Secondly, the *sui generis* protection of databases consti-tutes an obstacle to data re-usability for the purpose of developing AI as no extraction may occur without the prior authorisation of the database maker and no excep-tion may clearly benefit start-ups and SMEs in that re-gard. Furthermore, considering that the 'substantial investment' standard is arguably low, a significant amount of databases are afforded *sui generis* protection, which negatively impacts the re-usability of the data they contain.

Finally, the contractual protection of databases consti-tutes yet another obstacle to data re-usability as it gives data holders the possibility to further limit the re-use of the contents of their protected databases as well as to re-trieve their unprotected databases from the public domain without any clear restriction in scope or duration, which prevents start-ups and SMEs from re-using it for the pur-pose of developing AI.

Taking these findings into account, it must be assessed whether the initiatives put forward by the European Commission make it possible to properly tackle these lim-itations in order to facilitate the development of Al by start-ups and SMEs within the EU.

## III. The proposed solutions for data re-usability: data mining and data sharing in the EU

The first part identified legal and contractual limitations on the re-use of data by start-ups and SMEs in the context of the development of AI. In turn, this second part assesses the solutions for data re-usability proposed at the EU level, namely the new rules on data mining and the contractual approach to data sharing put forward by the European Commission.

### 1. The legal approach to data re-usability: regulating data mining in the Digital Single Market

As finally adopted, the DSM Directive introduces new rules which might have an impact on the re-use of data by start-ups and SMEs in the context of the development of AI, namely a harmonised definition of ‘text and data mining', a new exception for the purposes of scientific research and a new exception for the purposes of text and data mining.

**a) The notion of 'text and data mining'**

The DSM Directive defines the practice of text and data mining as 'any automated analytical technique aiming to analyse text and data in digital form' whose purpose is to 'generate information which includes but is not limited to patterns, trends and correlations'.¹²⁰

Concerning the first part of the definition, data proc-essing in the context of machine learning is undoubtedly an automated analytical technique in the sense that it relies on the analysis of digital data conducted by an algo-rithm on a relatively autonomous basis.¹²¹

Concerning the second part of the definition, the pur-pose of the data processing in the context of machine learning is indeed to generate information since it aims to ensure that the trained algorithm is able to produce valu-able insight in the form of additional data through the discovery of patterns, trends and correlations.¹²²

Based on the above considerations, the definition of text and data mining should be applicable to the process-ing that takes place in the context of AI training.

**b) The new exception for the purposes of scientific research**

The DSM Directive acknowledges that the practice of text and data mining may involve the reproduction of copy-righted materials as well as the extraction of contents from a protected database, which cannot be accomplished without the consent of the relevant rightholders in the ab-sence of any applicable exception.¹²³

To remedy this situation, the DSM Directive provides for a first exception to copyright and the *sui generis* right regarding reproductions and extractions made by re-search organisations and cultural heritage institutions in the context of text and data mining activities carried out on subject matter to which they have lawful access for the purposes of scientific research.¹²⁴

This exception is mandatory for Member States, which means that they will have no choice but to transpose it into national law.¹²⁵ Furthermore, this exception is unwaivable by contract,¹²⁶ which excludes the possibility for rightholders to limit its application by inserting a re-strictive clause in the terms of use of their lawfully accessi-ble databases.¹²⁷

The first category of beneficiaries covers 'research organisations',¹²⁸ which are defined as organisations whose primary goal is to conduct scientific research¹²⁹ ei-ther on a non-profit basis or on the basis of a public inter-est mission,¹³⁰ provided that they are not under the decisive influence of an undertaking which would enjoy the results of the scientific research on a preferential ba-sis.¹³¹ This last element has been criticised for potentially impeding the emergence of new applications in the private sector to the extent that start-ups and SMEs may not qualify as such organisations.¹³²

The second category of beneficiaries is limited to 'cul-tural heritage institutions', which are defined as publicly accessible libraries or museums, archives or film or audio heritage institutions.¹³³ Clearly, start-ups and SMEs may not qualify as such institutions either.¹³⁴

That being said, the DSM Directive ensures that re-search organisations and cultural heritage institutions may also benefit from the exception in the context of 'public-private partnerships', namely when they rely on private partners and their technological tools to carry out data mining activities.¹³⁵ This possibility was justified by the Union research policy, which encourages collabora-tions with the private sector.¹³⁶ Nevertheless, it has been argued that this option might be limited for start-ups and SMEs as such partnerships would be extremely time con-suming and difficult to handle for reduced teams.¹³⁷

In terms of material scope, the exception is limited to subject matter to which the beneficiaries have ‘lawful ac-cess'.¹³⁸ This concept is not defined as such in the DSM Directive but should cover access to content based on open access policies or contractual arrangements such as subscriptions and content freely available online.¹³⁹ As it pre-exists the DSM Directive, it must be understood alongside the clarifications and limitations identified by the case-law of the CJEU on the matter, which were dis-cussed above.¹⁴⁰

On top of that, the exception is further limited to data mining activities conducted for the purposes of 'scientific research', which refers to the natural and human scien-ces.¹⁴¹ Even in the context of a public-private partnership, it is not clear-cut whether the development of AI would qualify as research conducted in either of these two branches of science.¹⁴²

To conclude, the exception for the purposes of scien-tific research is of limited use for start-ups and SMEs as it was primarily designed to serve non-commercial research (rather than private sector innovation) aiming at the prog-ress of the natural and human sciences (rather than the development of new technologies such as AI).

**c) The new exception for the purposes of text and data mining**

To encourage innovation in the private sector as well,¹⁴³ the DSM Directive provides for a second exception to copyright and the *sui generis* right regarding reproduc-tions and extractions made of lawfully accessible subject matter for the purposes of text and data mining.¹⁴⁴

Like the first exception, it is mandatory for Member States, who will have to transpose it into national law.¹⁴⁵ However, unlike the first exception, it is not unwaivable by contract, which entails the possibility for rightholders to limit its application.¹⁴⁶

The DSM Directive does not attach a limited list of beneficiaries to this second exception: it is potentially lim-itless, extending beyond research organisations and cul-tural heritage institutions to include (e.g.) journalists, consumer associations, start-ups and SMEs.¹⁴⁷

The exception is applicable for the purposes of text and data mining, which are defined by the recitals as covering 'the development of new applications or technologies'.¹⁴⁸ It is thus clear that the purpose of developing Al may qualify as one of the purposes of text and data mining in the sense of the DSM Directive.

Furthermore, the exception covers subject matter accessed lawfully by the beneficiary, which notably includes contents that were 'made available to the public online'.¹⁴⁹ Arguably, a database protected by copyright and/or the *sui generis* right that was made available to the public online with the rightholder's consent, such as an electronic database either published on or taking the shape of a freely accessible website, could be lawfully re-used for the purpose of developing AI.¹⁵⁰

However, the DSM Directive also recognises that right-holders have the possibility to expressly reserve the use of the subject matter of the exception.¹⁵¹ When the right-holder has excluded the possibility to accomplish acts of reproduction and extraction on that database for the pur-poses of text and data mining, the exception may no lon-ger be invoked to legitimise the re-use of a protected database.¹⁵²

The reserve should be made 'in an appropriate man-ner',¹⁵³ which, in the case of content made publicly available online, refers to the use of machine-readable means.¹⁵⁴ The concept of 'machine readability' has been criticised for lacking clarity.¹⁵⁵ In its common meaning, it covers data that are structured and presented in a format that can be automatically processed by a computer.¹⁵⁶ In terms of practical implementation, examples of machine-readable means include metadata and the terms and conditions of a website or a service.¹⁵⁷ In the author's opinion, this provision should be interpreted restric-tively.¹⁵⁸ Finally, when the content was not made publicly available online, the rightholder may reserve the rights to mine the data by other means, such as contractual agree-ments or a unilateral declaration.¹⁵⁹

To conclude, the exception for the purposes of text and data mining may be useful for start-ups and SMEs in the context of the development of AI, although its application is left to the discretion of rightholders who have the op-tion to opt out of it.

Last but not least, these provisions only apply to databases protected by copyright and/or the *sui generis* right. In other terms, they have no impact on the con-tractual protection of databases that are not protected by copyright and the *sui generis* right. Therefore, it is important to assess the contractual approach to data sharing put forward by the European Commission in or-der to determine whether it took into account the remaining contractual limitations on data re-usability identified above.

### 2. The contractual approach to data re-usability: regulating data sharing in the Digital Single Market

Studying the approach to the sharing of non-personal data at the EU level requires (a) a presentation of the gen-eral principle of a free flow of non-personal data and (b) an analysis of the different initiatives put forward by the European Commission on data sharing.

**a) The general principle of a free flow of non-personal data**

A free flow of data refers to the situation where no legal barriers exist to the mobility of data across borders.¹⁶⁰ Historically, this scenario has been hampered by data localisation requirements, which restrict data flows from one state to another.¹⁶¹ In turn, these restric-tions have increased the cost of conducting cross-border business, in particular for start-ups and SMEs whose ac-tivities involve the storage and processing of data.¹⁶²

To remedy this situation, the Regulation establishing a framework for the free flow of non-personal data (herein-after 'Free Flow Regulation')¹⁶³ was adopted, which pro-hibits data localisation requirements in the EU except in exceptional circumstances on grounds of public secu-rity and in compliance with the principle of proportionality.¹⁶⁴

The adoption of the Free Flow Regulation was pre-sented as an important step for the realisation of the Digital Single Market, which would reduce the costs of starting a business and help start-ups and SMEs in the EU speed up their pace of innovation.¹⁶⁵ With this framework in mind, the next paragraph focuses on the initiatives put forward by the European Commission concerning the conclusion of data shar-ing agreements.

**b) The specific rules on public and private sector data sharing**

To this day, there is no comprehensive EU legal frame-work governing the sharing of data, which implies that data exchanges are merely handled on a contractual ba-sis.¹⁶⁶ Building on the principle of a free flow of data, the European Commission has put forward initiatives on data sharing whose impact must be assessed from the per-spective of start-ups and SMEs.¹⁶⁷

**(a) Public sector data sharing**

Data sharing agreements may be concluded in the context of the relationship between public entities and private companies (also called the 'Government-to-Business' or 'G2B' context).¹⁶⁸

At first, pursuant to the Directive on the re-use of pub-lic sector information (hereinafter ‘PSI Directive’),¹⁶⁹ only public sector bodies were under the obligation to allow the re-use of accessible public sector datasets.¹⁷⁰

Recently, the PSI Directive was repealed and replaced by the Directive on open data and the re-use of public sec-tor information (hereinafter 'Open Data Directive'),¹⁷¹ which now also applies to public undertakings that have made their data available.¹⁷²

Where a public undertaking has decided to make a doc-ument available for re-use (be it for free or at a certain cost), the re-use conditions determined by the public undertakings must be non-discriminatory and justified by a public interest objective without unnecessarily restrict-ing the possibilities for re-use and without restricting competition.¹⁷³

An important limitation is that they are without preju-dice to third parties' intellectual property rights, which re-fer only to copyright and related rights, such as the *sui generis* right.¹⁷⁴ As a consequence, if a third party holds such a right in the document, the rules are not applicable. Conversely, if the public entity itself holds such a right, it has the obligation to licence its re-use under the afore-mentioned conditions.¹⁷⁵

Interestingly, the Open Data Directive further man-dates that 'high-value datasets' are made available. The list of these datasets will be drawn by the European Commission on the basis of thematic categories and the assessment of several criteria, including their potential to benefit start-ups and SMEs.¹⁷⁶

To conclude, the new rules might prove beneficial for start-ups and SMEs as they are likely to ensure that more public sector data are made available for re-use under non-discriminatory conditions. This regulates the practice of public sector data sharing to a certain extent and reduces the possibility for public sector data holders to rely on restrictive contractual clauses to further limit the re-use of their databases.

**(b) Private sector data sharing**

Data sharing agreements may also be concluded in the context of the relationship between commercial entities themselves (also called the ‘Business-to-Business' or 'B2B' context).¹⁷⁷

In that respect, after taking into account the call for caution voiced by stakeholders, the European Commission deemed the adoption of regulatory measures inappropriate at this stage.¹⁷⁸ Therefore, it renounced its previous proposal of having a new right of ownership of the data itself and issued instead a non-binding guidance on private sector data sharing, in which the freedom of contract was set as a cornerstone.¹⁷⁹

To begin with, this guidance puts forward recommen-dations to facilitate the preparation of a private sector data sharing agreement, in the course of which businesses are advised to describe the data covered, the persons enti-tled to access it and the ways in which it may be re-used.¹⁸⁰ However, it does not address situations where the request for re-use is rejected by the data holder.¹⁸¹ Be that as it may, the European Commission indicated that it could envisage further actions in the future, such as the adoption of codes of conduct.¹⁸²

Following its release, the non-binding character of this guidance has been criticised for lacking any impact on data sharing in practice.¹⁸³ In sum, it appears that the European Commission has put forward a rather modest approach to private sector data sharing, limited to a non-binding guidance which does not provide appropriate sol-utions to the contractual limitations on data re-usability encountered by start-ups and SMEs.

**c) Conclusion**

The purpose of this section was to determine whether the data sharing initiatives put forward by the European Commission brought any improvement concerning the contractual limitations on data re-usability encountered in practice by start-ups and SMEs.

On the one hand, the Open Data Directive is promising with regard to public sector data as it mandates the mak-ing available and re-usability of high value datasets and compels public undertakings to respect a principle of non-discrimination when it comes to sharing their data.

On the other hand, the guidance provided by the European Commission is disappointing with regard to private sector data as it merely promotes the freedom of contract without tackling the contractual limitations on data re-use encountered in practice. In that respect, it appears that restrictive clauses may still be relied on by data holders to limit the re-use of their unprotected data-bases. Taking into account the needs of start-ups and SMEs, it appears necessary to present recommendations to improve the existing framework.

## IV. The way forward: recommendations for a more balanced framework

This third part puts forward recommendations which are divided into two categories, namely those concerning the legal limitations on data re-usability and those concerning the contractual limitations on data re-usability.

### 1. Preliminary remarks on the quest for a 'fair balance'

The Charter of Fundamental Rights guarantees the right to freedom of information, according to which informa-tion should remain free from appropriation.¹⁸⁴ Based on this principle, from an economic perspective, data are generally considered common goods, which cannot be objects of property.¹⁸⁵ The stance of the European Commission on promoting the free flow of data through-out the EU and its rejection of the idea of a data pro-ducer's right are in conformity with this principle.¹⁸⁶

In that regard, it bears reminding that copyright law was designed to promote, rather than impede, the free movement of information. Indeed, when a work is pro-tected by copyright based on the originality standard, the information contained in the work remains freely avail-able in order to strike an appropriate balance between the right to (intellectual) property and the right to freedom of information, equally protected under the Charter of Fundamental Rights.¹⁸⁷

The need to strike a fair balance between fundamental rights has been a long-standing concern of the EU legisla-tor, which has been echoed by the CJEU in its recent case-law on copyright and related rights.¹⁸⁸ This leads one to wonder whether that balance was appropriately struck at the time of the adoption of the Database Directive, which introduced the *sui generis* right in the EU.

From the outset, the *sui generis* right has been criticised for favouring the interests of database makers over the interests of data users.¹⁸⁹ In spite of being more compre-hensive in terms of its scope of protection, the *sui generis* right was paradoxically accompanied by fewer and nar-rower exceptions than copyright, which remain optional for Member States to implement.¹⁹⁰

Since the adoption of the Database Directive, the evolu-tion of the ways data are produced and gathered (such as in the case of machine-generated data) has led to an in-crease in the applications of the *sui generis* right, in line with the findings of the CJEU on this matter.¹⁹¹ On the other hand, the emergence of innovative data analysis techniques (such as web scraping and data mining) has lent a growing sense of legal uncertainty concerning what uses may or may not be conducted with regard to avail-able online datasets.¹⁹²

In theory, the Database Directive may distinguish be-tween the data and the database, but in practice the European Commission admitted that it comes danger-ously close to providing for ownership of the data itself, which entitles the rightholder to exercise some control over the information contained in the database.¹⁹³ In the context of the recent evaluation of the Database Directive, it was acknowledged that an extension of the exceptions might be regarded as highly desirable in order to accommodate the fundamental rights protected by the Charter of Fundamental Rights.¹⁹⁴

This seems further confirmed by the lack of evidence that the *sui generis* right has had any effect on the produc-tion of databases in the EU.¹⁹⁵ Compared to data protec-tion law, which restricts access to information based on the legitimate interests of the data subject and his right to privacy, and trade secrets, which adequately ensure the protection of the commercial interests of the rightholder provided the information is indeed kept secret, the *sui ge-neris* right seems to lack a proper justification for the extensive restrictions it puts on the right to freedom of information.¹⁹⁶

In the quest for a fair balance between the right to free-dom of information, which supports the interests of data users to access and re-use data, and the right to intellec-tual property, which supports the interests of database makers in restricting access to and re-use of their data-base, it is useful to put forward recommendations that take due account of the current weaknesses of the *sui ge-neris* right.

### 2. Towards a more balanced framework on the legal protection of databases

As a reminder, the copyright protection of databases is unlikely to pose too many problems in practice for start-ups and SMEs, as the 'originality' threshold is arguably difficult to attain for most available databases.¹⁹⁷

Conversely, the *sui generis* protection of databases has proven more problematic in terms of data re-usability since the 'substantial investment' threshold is arguably easier to attain and the initial term of protection can be renewed for a potentially indefinite period of time.¹⁹⁸

In that regard, the text and data mining exceptions in-troduced by the DSM Directive have not restored the bal-ance in favour of data users other than research organisations and cultural heritage institutions.¹⁹⁹ Arguably, this seems hardly compatible with the purpose of the DSM Directive, which was to reconcile the high level of protection afforded to rightholders with innova-tion and ensure legal certainty with regard to new types of uses permitted by innovative digital technologies.²⁰⁰

To fix this situation, two potential solutions may be put forward in order to improve the existing legal frame-work: extending the scope of the exception for the pur-poses of text and data mining or, alternatively, reforming the *sui generis* right.

**a) The extension of the text and data mining exception**

A first potential solution would be to repeal para. 3 of Art. 4 of the DSM Directive, which provides for the possi-bility for rightholders to opt out of the exception for the purposes of text and data mining, and to add a reference to Art. 4 in Art. 7(1) of the DSM Directive in order to make the exception unwaivable by rightholders.

However, this seemingly simple solution might prove problematic with regard to the so-called 'three-step test',²⁰¹ which requires that any exception to copyright be limited to certain special cases that do not conflict with the normal exploitation of the work and do not unreason-ably prejudice the legitimate interests of the author.

In any case, a solution to overcome that potential hur-dle would be to maintain the possibility for copyright holders to opt out of the exception while excluding that possibility for *sui generis* right holders, since the three-step test is only applicable in the realm of copyright law and must not be taken into account concerning the excep-tions to the *sui generis* right.

One argument against this potential solution would be that the Database Directive contains traces of the three-step test in that it prevents the lawful user of a protected database from performing acts which conflict with the normal exploitation of that database or unreasonably prejudice the legitimate interest of the rightholder.²⁰² Accordingly, the three-step test would have to be applied when revising the exceptions to the *sui generis* right.

A counterargument may be found in the differences be-tween the copyright regime and the *sui generis* regime. In the realm of copyright, the three-step test has a general application with regard to the exceptions to the author's rights.²⁰³ Conversely, the core elements of the three-step test found in the Database Directive are limited to situa-tions where a lawful user extracts or re-utilises insubstan-tial parts of a protected database, which arguably reduces the scope of application of these core elements.²⁰⁴ In any case, the study in support of the evaluation of the Database Directive has expressed doubts as to whether this provision was 'ever relevant', which makes the appli-cation of the three-step test questionable with regard to the *sui generis* right.²⁰⁵

Based on these considerations, one might argue that the three-step test should not be taken into account for the purpose of extending an existing exception to the *sui generis* right.

**b) The reform of the sui generis right**

Rather than providing for yet another exception to the *sui generis* protection, a somewhat more ambitious solution would be to bring substantial changes to the *sui generis* right itself or, at the most extreme, to contemplate its complete removal.

**(a) The modification of the sui generis right**

A first possibility would be to subject the benefit of the *sui generis* right to a registration procedure at the EUIPO.²⁰⁶

The registration would be automatic upon payment of the registration fees and provide for an initial term of pro-tection of five years.²⁰⁷ In turn, the renewal of the protec-tion would happen on a yearly basis upon the condition of payment of renewal fees, which would increase over time and be limited to a maximum period of fifteen years.²⁰⁸

Arguably, this registration procedure would facilitate the distinction between protected and unprotected data-bases and avoid potential data 'lock ups' resulting from indefinitely renewed terms of protection.²⁰⁹

In this new form, however, the *sui generis* right might bring new disadvantages in the context of the data econ-omy. For one thing, the registration procedure might prove impractical, especially with regard to the object of the registration itself: a digital database may be hard to identify and disclose in practice, especially when it has a dynamic nature, meaning that its content is automatically updated.²¹⁰

Furthermore, the rightholder might prefer to keep his database undisclosed in order to protect his legitimate commercial interests, in which case the database would not benefit from this new form of *sui generis* protec-tion.²¹¹ The registration procedure might also lead to higher transaction costs as database makers might rely on the proof of registration to justify stricter conditions of access and re-use to the detriment of data users. In order to ensure that this registration procedure does not prove counterproductive to its goal, sector-specific data access rights might be designed in a way that safeguards a fair balance between the interests of the rightholder and the right to freedom of information.²¹²

**(b) The abolition of the sui generis right**

Beyond mere modifications, a more radical solution would be to consider the abolition of the *sui generis* right altogether.²¹³

Arguably, the *sui generis* right comes close to granting ownership of the data itself, which is hard to reconcile with the impulse given by the European Commission in abandoning the idea of a right of data ownership.²¹⁴ Such a right would indeed have the potential to hinder innova-tion and growth in the Digital Single Market and does not fit well with the focus of the European Commission on the organisation of access to existing datasets.²¹⁵

More fundamentally, the economic justification behind the adoption of the Database Directive, which lies at the heart of the *sui generis* right, has been repeatedly called into question.

In its first evaluation, the European Commission con-cluded that, despite an effective harmonisation of the *sui generis* aspects of the legal protection of databases, the Database Directive had no proven impact on the produc-tion of databases in the European Union.²¹⁶

In its second evaluation, the European Commission confirmed its findings, concluding that the Database Directive had not proven to create more incentives to in-vest in the production of databases in the European Union and had not succeeded in establishing a proper bal-ance between the interests of data holders and data users.²¹⁷

In this regard, the majority of respondents to the public consultations expressed the view that the Database Directive is not adequate in the context of the data econ-omy.²¹⁸ Based on the above considerations, it can be ar-gued that the *sui generis* right fails to strike a proper balance with the right of freedom of information as it is likely to aggravate the problem of data access and re-use to the ultimate detriment of innovation within the Digital Single Market.²¹⁹

**c) Conclusion**

There are essentially three ways to achieve a more bal-anced framework for the legal protection of databases in the EU that would take into account the innovative poten-tial of start-ups and SMEs in the context of the develop-ment of AI: extending the scope of the exception for the purposes of text and data mining, bringing appropriate modifications to the *sui generis* right or abolishing it altogether.

However, none of these options would suffice to pro-vide a sustainable solution in terms of data re-usability since data holders would still have the possibility to limit the re-use of their unprotected databases by way of re-strictive contractual clauses. Therefore, the next section puts forward recommendations to alleviate the contrac-tual limitations on data re-usability.

### 3. Towards a more balanced framework for the contractual protection of databases

As explained above, contracts are the main form of data-base protection which data holders rely on in order to restrict the re-use of their databases, in conformity with the findings of the CJEU in the *Ryanair* judgment.²²⁰

As a matter of fact, the study conducted in support of the second evaluation of the Database Directive found ev-idence that the *Ryanair* judgment has negatively impacted the goals pursued by that Directive in that it has led to more restrictive contractual terms being drafted and to variation in their enforceability across the EU.²²¹

An appropriate way to restore the balance between the interests of data holders and data users would be (1) to in-troduce a new right to re-use the non-personal data con-tained in databases that have been disclosed by data holders, an approach which, provided that some addi-tional research is first conducted on the topic, might be complemented at a later stage by (2) the introduction of carefully tailored data access rights in order to reconcile the protection of databases with the right to freedom of information.

**a) The introduction of a new data user right**

Arguably, the possibility for data holders to rely on terms and conditions to further limit the possibilities of data re-use reinforces the *de facto* ownership they have over their unprotected databases, which is hard to reconcile with the impulse given by the European Commission in aban-doning the idea of a right of data ownership.²²²

Despite its potential to threaten the proper functioning of the Digital Single Market and the integrity of the public domain, this contractual practice has nevertheless not been called into question by any of the legal initiatives considered in the present paper.²²³

First, it would be conceivable to introduce a right to re-use the non-personal data contained in databases which have been disclosed by or with the consent of the data holder. Most notably, this right would find many applica-tions in the digital environment, where a tremendous amount of websites may be accessed without restrictions.

Optimally, this right would be unwaivable to ensure that data holders could no longer limit the possibility for data users to re-use their unprotected databases. In other terms, the *Ryanair* judgment could no longer be invoked by data holders in order to limit the re-usability of their unprotected database by means of restrictive clauses inserted in their terms and conditions.²²⁴

On the other hand, this right should be without preju-dice to existing IP rights in order to limit its application to unprotected databases. This right would effectively ensure that all databases that do not meet the standards of copy-right and the *sui generis* right remain freely re-usable by data users.

Like the second text and data mining exception re-cently adopted in the DSM Directive, this right should be limitless in terms of potential beneficiaries to ensure that it applies identically to everyone, including start-ups and SMEs. Combined with the proposed extension of that text and data mining exception, this new right would en-sure that data mining activities may take place with re-gard to disclosed databases, even when protected by copyright, the *sui generis* right or restrictive terms and conditions.

Finally, since this new right would only apply to unpro-tected databases, it would hardly belong in the Database Directive, which regulates the legal protection of data-bases only and does not apply to unprotected data-bases.²²⁵ It would therefore be more adequate to include it in a yet-to-be-invented, comprehensive piece of EU sec-ondary legislation, be it in the form of a directive or a reg-ulation, that would lay down binding rules on private sector data sharing and put adequate limits on the unfair contractual practices taking place in that respect.²²⁶

To conclude, while bilateral contracts may be an ap-propriate tool to determine the re-usability of protected databases, unilateral terms and conditions imposed by data holders to restrict the re-usability of their unpro-tected databases should not be enforceable against data users. In the light of the many criticisms of the *Ryanair* judgment, this proposed user right seems adequate to re-store the integrity of the public domain, taking due ac-count of the fundamental principle of freedom of information as well as the interests of data users in the digital environment.

**b) The introduction of a new data access regime**

Beyond a new right to re-use data, a more ambitious pro-posal would consist in a new right to access undisclosed data in order to re-use it. Indeed, it is foreseeable that the data needed to develop new forms of AI will often be locked behind technical protection measures or within companies' walls, protected by the *de facto* ownership of the data holder.²²⁷

Access to data is of fundamental importance to the well-being of individuals and to the development of soci-ety as a whole.²²⁸ From the perspective of individuals, ac-cess to data is justified by the right to self-determination and the broader principle of freedom of information.²²⁹ From a market perspective, data access rights are per-ceived as desirable to avoid the 'monopolisation' of data.²³⁰

Arguably, data access rights would solve the imbalance in power between market operators, a result that competi-tion law fails to achieve on its own.²³¹ According to this view, data access rights would ultimately be beneficial for societal development as they would foster possibilities of data-driven innovation that include smaller businesses.²³²

The topic of data access rights is contentious and calls for caution: if such rights seem beneficial for data users in terms of the prospect of innovation, these apparent bene-fits must be balanced against the detrimental effects they would have for data holders.²³³ Furthermore, it is often argued that such rights are not needed for the reason that an unfair refusal of access may be adequately handled un-der competition law.²³⁴

At this stage, more questions than answers exist con-cerning the design of such access rights: which beneficia-ries should be considered, which data should be covered, which conditions should be imposed, which form should the access request take and which technical measures should be set in place to ensure the safety and confidenti-ality of the data accessed?²³⁵

In the case of G2B data sharing, data access rights could be designed in a way that applies not only to public entities but also to private undertakings that receive state subsidies or fulfil a mission of public interest.²³⁶ Such en-tities would then be under the obligation to provide the public interest data collected through their activity to the authority in a machine-readable format so that innovative businesses may access and re-use them in the context of data analysis and data mining activities.²³⁷

In the case of B2B data sharing, data access could be fa-cilitated on a contractual basis, following the guidance of the European Commission on the matter.²³⁸ In that con-text, adequate limits would be set with regard to the prin-ciple of freedom of contract. A first way to achieve that goal would be to put in place a regime of access against remuneration inspired by the concept of FRAND terms.²³⁹ The contractual approach to data sharing could also be complemented by the adoption of unwaivable rules on Unfair Contract Terms (UCT), which would ap-ply when an imbalance in power exists between the data holder and the data user in the context of data sharing agreements.²⁴⁰

As it is not yet clear how data markets will evolve in the future, it is safe to consider that a regulated approach would only be adequate if the particularities of each sector are taken into account. A legally binding framework on data access should therefore be complemented at the differ-ent levels of the industry on the basis of self-regulation.

It is not the place of the present article to assess whether the introduction of access rights would be rele-vant for data users when balanced with the interests of data holders. Depending on the results of further research on the topic, data access rights might be considered as useful complements in the context of a more legally secure framework for private sector data sharing, which would be beneficial for innovation, especially in the field of AI.

## V. Conclusion

In the context of artificial intelligence, data constitute the raw material enabling algorithms to learn how to achieve specific tasks. Due to their limited size and resources, however, start-ups and SMEs lack appropriate amounts of re-usable data to properly innovate in that field.

The European Commission has acknowledged this re-ality in pursuing EU-level strategies that aim to create a data economy in which the creation of new AI technolo-gies by innovative start-ups and SMEs is encouraged. In spite of these goals, the framework on the protection of databases remains characterised by significant limitations on data re-usability, which impedes the development of AI in the EU.

Concerning the legal protection of databases, the pro-cess of developing AI may entail several infringements on the copyright and *sui generis* protection of available data-bases in the form of unauthorised reproductions and extractions which cannot be properly covered by the exceptions listed in the Database Directive.

To alleviate this burden, the DSM Directive has intro-duced two new exceptions to copyright and the *sui ge-neris* right in favour of the practice of text and data mining. However, the result is disappointing since start-ups and SMEs may not benefit from the exception for the purposes of scientific research outside of public-private partnerships and cannot rely on the exception for the purposes of text and data mining when rightholders have reserved the use of their databases.

In the face of the remaining legal limitations on data re-usability, the present paper recommends three potential ways to improve the existing framework, namely the ex-tension of the scope of the new exception for the purposes of text and data mining, a significant modification of the *sui generis* right or, at the most extreme, its complete abolition.

Concerning the contractual protection of databases, the process of developing AI may be impeded by restric-tive clauses inserted by data holders in the terms and con-ditions of their unprotected databases made available online, whose enforceability is left to national courts and varies from one Member State to the other.

Taking these obstacles into account, the data sharing ini-tiatives put forward by the European Commission are promising with regard to public sector data since they have led to the inclusion of public undertakings in the scope of the Open Data Directive, which now mandates that high-value datasets are made available and that non-discriminatory conditions of re-use are respected. Conversely, these initiatives are disappointing with regard to private sector data since they merely acknowledge the contractual freedom of private entities without introducing any relevant provision with regard to data re-usability.

In turn, the present paper recommends the introduction of an unwaivable right to re-use the non-personal data contained in available databases as an appropriate first step towards the creation of a more balanced framework on data re-usability. Beyond that first step, further re-search is needed on the adequacy of introducing unwaiv-able data access rights to complement this framework.

As this paper has shown, the rules on the legal protec-tion of databases are outdated and should be further amended in order to accommodate the digital economy that the European Commission intends to make a reality. Conversely, the rules applicable to the contractual practi-ces surrounding the re-use of data are quite recent and should be complemented in order to tackle the obstacles faced by data users such as start-ups and SMEs in the context of the development of AI.
