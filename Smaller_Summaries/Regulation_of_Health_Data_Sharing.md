Module 2: Regulation of Health Data Sharing (Exam Summary)
I. The European Health Data Space (EHDS) Framework
A. Objectives and Context
The European Health Data Space (EHDS) is a cornerstone of the European Health Union and the first common EU data space dedicated to a specific sector, forming part of the broader European strategy for data [1, 2]. The legislative proposal for the EHDS Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2025/327) was issued by the European Commission in May 2022 and published in February 2025 [2-4].
The EHDS aims to achieve two ambitious goals [2, 3, 5-7]:
1. Primary Use: To grant citizens increased access to and control over their (electronic) health data across the EU [3, 8].
2. Secondary Use: To facilitate health data re-use for research, innovation, policymaking, and regulatory activities [3, 9].
The impetus for the EHDS stemmed from several factors [10-12]:
• An increased focus on promoting public health at the EU level following the COVID-19 pandemic, which highlighted the urgent need for timely access to quality electronic health data for health threat preparedness and response [12-14].
• A recognition that previous purely voluntary programmes (like the Cross-Border Healthcare Directive (CBHC)) were ineffective, resulting in fragmentation and low interoperability of digital health systems [10, 15-17].
• The practical difficulty faced by patients in exercising their existing GDPR rights in the health sector [10, 11].
B. Definition of Health Data
Electronic health data is a broad category encompassing both personal and non-personal electronic health data [18]. It includes [19, 20]:
• Genetic data and data concerning health (personal electronic health data) [18, 21].
• Inferred and derived data, such as diagnostics, tests, and medical examinations [19].
• Data determinants of health, such as behavior, environment, and social factors [19].
• Data from wellness applications automatically generated through medical devices [22, 23].

II. The Dual Regulatory Regime: Primary vs. Secondary Use
The EHDS establishes distinct requirements for the two main uses of data [24, 25].
Feature
Primary Use (Healthcare Delivery) [26]
Secondary Use (Re-use for Broader Purposes) [26]
Purpose
Provision of healthcare, assessment, treatment, prescription, and related administrative/reimbursement services [26].
Research, innovation, policy-making, regulatory activities, education, patient safety, or personalized medicine [27-29].
Economic Rationale
Economies of scope (data re-use at low marginal cost) and reducing information asymmetry between providers/patients [30-32].
Efficiency gains from economies of scale and scope through aggregation of fragmented datasets [33, 34].
Infrastructure
MyHealth@EU (mandatory cross-border infrastructure for exchange) [35].
HealthData@EU (cross-border infrastructure for aggregation) [36].
Mechanism
Enhanced patient rights (access, portability, control) enforced via Digital Health Authorities [37, 38].
Data Permit Scheme managed by Health Data Access Bodies (HDABs) [39, 40].
A. Primary Use: Enhancing Patient Rights (Chapter II)
The EHDS complements and specifies existing GDPR rights (particularly access and portability) for electronic health data [41-43].
1. Access and Portability: Natural persons have the right to immediate, free-of-charge access to their data, and the right to download and transmit electronic copies, at least for priority categories [8, 37, 44, 45].
    ◦ Scope Extension: This right extends to inferred data (like diagnostics) and requires data transmission in an interoperable format, addressing limitations of GDPR Article 20 portability [25, 43, 46].
    ◦ Priority Categories: Includes patient summaries, electronic prescriptions, electronic dispensations, medical imaging/reports, medical test results, and discharge reports [47, 48].
2. Control: Individuals gain more control, including the ability to [8, 49]:
    ◦ Add information to their EHRs (must be clearly distinguishable) [49].
    ◦ Request online rectification of incorrect data [50].
    ◦ Restrict access (selective sharing) to parts of their data for health professionals/providers [51]. However, access restrictions can be overridden in life-threatening emergency situations to protect vital interests [52, 53].
3. Opt-Out (Primary Use): Member States may allow a right to opt out from access to EHR data via EHDS services (Articles 4 and 12). If exercised, the data would not be available through EHDS services, though providers would still document treatment nationally [54, 55].
4. Health Professionals' Access: Health professionals gain access to relevant and necessary patient data through Health Professional Access Services to ensure continuity of care, avoiding duplication and errors [56, 57].
B. Secondary Use: Mandatory Data Sharing (Chapter IV)
1. Mandatory Provision of Data: Health data holders (including healthcare providers, insurers, researchers, wellness application developers, and Union bodies) must make specific categories of electronic health data available to HDABs upon request [58-60].
    ◦ Exemptions: Natural persons and microenterprises are generally exempt from mandatory data holder obligations, though Member States may apply these obligations to them in national law [61, 62].
2. Data Access Bodies (HDABs): Each Member State must designate one or more HDABs responsible for managing access, issuing permits, monitoring compliance, and processing/preparing data [40, 63].
    ◦ Function: They act as a gatekeeper to ensure data access applications meet the legal criteria (purpose, necessity, proportionality) and safeguards [64, 65].
    ◦ Procedure: Applicants submit a Health Data Access Application, and the HDAB issues a Data Permit (or refuses the application) within three months (or two months for public sector bodies/Union institutions under an accelerated procedure) [66-68].
3. Data Protection and Security: Access must occur in a Secure Processing Environment (SPE) [69, 70].
    ◦ Data Minimization: HDABs must provide data in anonymised format if possible. If not, data is provided in pseudonymised format, with the information necessary to reverse pseudonymization held only by the HDAB or a trusted third party [71, 72].
    ◦ Prohibitions: Health data users are prohibited from using data for: taking detrimental decisions against individuals (e.g., modifying insurance premiums, excluding persons from contracts) [73]; advertising or marketing activities [74]; developing products that may harm individuals (e.g., illicit drugs, weaponry, addiction-creating products) [74]; or re-identifying or attempting to re-identify natural persons [75, 76].
    ◦ Enforcement: Violations of prohibited uses or re-identification attempts are subject to high administrative fines (up to €20,000,000 or 4% of worldwide annual turnover) [77]. HDABs also have the power to revoke data permits or exclude users for up to five years [78, 79].
4. Opt-Out (Secondary Use): Natural persons have the right to opt out from secondary processing at any time, reversibly, without providing a reason [80].
    ◦ Public Interest Override: A Member State may provide an exception to the opt-out right if the data is needed by a public sector body (or entity acting on its behalf) for key public interest purposes, such as protection against serious cross-border threats to health or scientific research for important reasons of public interest [61, 81].

III. Interoperability and Governance
A. Data Standards and Infrastructure
1. Interoperability Mandate (Primary Use): The EHDS requires mandatory technical specifications for the European electronic health record exchange format (EEHRxF) [82]. Where personal electronic health data are transmitted by automated means, the receiving provider must accept the format and be able to read it [83].
2. EHR System Requirements: Manufacturers of EHR systems must comply with essential requirements for interoperability, security, and logging (Annex II) [84, 85]. This requires a mandatory conformity self-assessment (CE marking) concerning two mandatory harmonized software components (interoperability and logging components) [86, 87].
3. Interoperability Standard: To facilitate full interoperability, one recommendation is that the Commission ensure a single prominent European or international health data standard, such as Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR), is adopted and used widely [88-90].
4. Governance Structures:
    ◦ Digital Health Authorities (DHAs): Responsible for implementing and enforcing primary use (Chapter II/III) at the national level [38].
    ◦ Health Data Access Bodies (HDABs): Responsible for implementing and enforcing secondary use (Chapter IV) [63].
    ◦ EHDS Board: Established to facilitate cooperation and consistent application across Member States, composed of representatives for both primary and secondary use [91, 92].
B. Implementation Timeline
The EHDS Regulation (EU) 2025/327 applies from 26 March 2027 [93]. Full implementation is structured in stages [94]:
• March 2029: Application deadline for core primary data categories (Patient Summaries, ePrescriptions/eDispensations) and corresponding EHR system requirements [93, 94]. Secondary use rules also begin applying for most data categories [94].
• March 2031: Application deadline for complex primary data categories (medical images, lab results, discharge reports) [93, 94]. Secondary use rules apply to remaining categories (e.g., genomic data) [94].

IV. Critical Analysis and Challenges (Exam Focus)
A. Legal and Regulatory Coherence
The EHDS introduces complex interactions and potential conflicts with existing EU legislation [95, 96].

EHDS requires no fees for primary data transfers, overriding DA provisions that allow data holders to charge for product data transfer [97, 98].

EHDS mandates EHR interoperability standards and sets patient rights, requiring substantial changes in diverse national health data laws and practices [95, 100, 101].
B. Critical Concerns Raised by Experts
1. Erosion of Patient Control: Lowering standards related to information provision and discouraging reliance on explicit consent (in favor of opt-out mechanisms and public interest overrides) may diminish individual control and informational self-determination [102-104].
2. Risk to Public Trust: Stimulating the economy by granting broad access to citizens’ health data could potentially backfire and have detrimental effects on public trust in medical research, especially if the vast scope of sharing is perceived as posing high risks without clear public benefits [105, 106].
3. Big Tech and Value Leakage: Under broad "general interest" definitions, tech corporations are poised to have unobstructed access to the EHDS [107]. A major concern is ensuring that the value generated from this public data flows back to the public sector, preventing scenarios where proprietary algorithms trained on public data are sold back to public health systems [108, 109].
4. Implementation Deficit and Burden: The EHDS risks placing an unsustainable administrative burden on Health Data Access Bodies (HDABs) (with nearly two dozen mandated duties) and fails to adequately incentivize health professionals to participate fully in data creation and sharing [110-113].
5. Data Structure and Privacy: While centralized EU-level infrastructure is planned (e.g., Commission operating a core platform for HealthData@EU) [114], this risks placing a heavy burden on the HDABs and raising privacy concerns [115, 116]. Experts recommend clarifying flexibility for decentralized/federated data architectures (bringing algorithms to data, rather than data to algorithms) to mitigate privacy risks (e.g., mass leakage) and potentially reduce infrastructure costs [111, 117-119].
C. Recommendations for Improvement (Policy Adjustments)
Experts propose several recommendations [119]:
• HDAB Functionality: Review the authority and heavy responsibilities (Articles 37 and 39) of the HDABs and consider expanding exemptions to the data permit procedure to reduce administrative friction [111, 119].
• Clarity on Centralization: Clarify the degree to which centralized versus decentralized/federated infrastructure is permissible [118, 119].
• Legal Clarity: Streamline the drafting of permissible and prohibited secondary uses to improve legal certainty, particularly regarding the prohibitions applied to insurance companies and marketing [120, 121].
• Incentives: Increase incentives for health professionals to participate in data creation and sharing [121].
• Trade Secrets: Clarify the meaning and protection of trade secrets concerning health service providers when data is co-generated by patients and providers [120, 122].
