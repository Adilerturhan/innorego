Module 3 - Data & Regulation of Competition: Case Analysis
The EU competition law regime rests on three main pillars: restrictive agreements (Article 101 TFEU), abuse of market power (Article 102 TFEU), and merger control (EC Merger Control Regulation) [1-6].
Case 1: Commission v Microsoft
Scenario: Microsoft introduces a corporate version of Windows that only fully supports its own Office software, excluding performance and compatibility software from competitors (Google Docs, Libre Office) [7].
Competition Tool Applied: Abuse of Dominant Position (Article 102 TFEU) [1, 3, 8, 9].
Analysis of Abuse of Dominance (Article 102 TFEU)
The legal test for establishing an abuse of a dominant position typically requires five steps [9]:
Criteria
Application and Explanation
Supporting Sources
1. Undertaking
Met. An undertaking is any economic entity engaged in commercial activity [9-11]. Microsoft is an economic entity engaged in commercial activities and is therefore an undertaking [10].
[9, 10]
2. Dominant Position
Met (Assumed). A dominant position exists when a competitor can act independently on the market [9, 12, 13]. Microsoft can exclude other service providers in the relevant product market independently, suggesting it holds a dominant position [12]. A dominant company has a special responsibility not to allow its conduct to impair genuine competition [13, 14].
[12-14]
3. Abuse
Met. Although there is no single definition of abuse [12], the behavior here is the exclusion of competitors from the office-related software market [12]. This exclusionary conduct involves degrading performance and compatibility for rivals (like Google Docs and Libre Office) [7, 12]. Historically, the refusal to disclose interface information (data/know-how) by Microsoft was deemed an abuse because it hindered competitors from offering interoperable products [15-18].
[7, 12, 16-18]
4. Substantial Part of Internal Market
Met. The market must be distorted in a substantial part of the internal market [14, 19]. The criteria is fulfilled even if only one Member State is sufficiently affected [10, 14, 19]. The case concerns corporate offices in the EU, meaning the EU market or a substantial part of it is affected [7, 10].
[10, 14, 19]
5. No Objective Justification
Met. An anti-competitive behavior may be justified if the gains for the consumer outweigh the harm for the market [19]. Here, the action is not justified because users of competitor software (Libre Office or Google Docs) cannot use these in their office computers, meaning the alleged gains are not translated to the consumer [19].
[19]
Conclusion: The action at hand is an abuse of a dominant position and therefore constitutes a violation of Article 102 TFEU [8].
Case 2: Apple/Netflix
Scenario: Apple proposes to acquire 55% of Netflix shares, arguing that the concentration will lead to significant efficiencies and innovation benefits in the digital entertainment market [8].
Competition Tool Applied: Merger Control (Regulation (EC) No 139/2004 – EUMR) [1, 4-6].
Analysis of Merger Control
The European Commission (EC) must be notified of planned mergers if the resulting company exceeds certain turnover thresholds (Community dimension) [13, 20]. Merger control is a preventive tool used to identify and prohibit concentrations that would significantly impede effective competition (SIEC) before the merger is implemented [4, 5, 21, 22].
The EC must assess the following criteria [20, 22, 23]:
1. Concentration: This is met through the Acquisition of 55% of Netflix shares by Apple [22, 23].
2. EU Dimension: This relates to turnover thresholds [24]. The EC assumes the requirement is met, given the huge turnover generated by both Apple and Netflix worldwide and in the EU [23].
3. Significantly Impede Effective Competition (SIEC): The Commission must decide if the merger significantly distorts competition [13, 23]. This analysis considers whether the concentration risks creating or strengthening a dominant position [13]. As Apple is known for its extensive ecosystem (hardware, App Store, Apple TV+), the EC would assess the potential for competitive harm, possibly through foreclosure or leveraging effects (as this appears to be a conglomerate merger) [8, 25].
4. Substantial Part of the Internal Market: The impediment must be in the internal market or a substantial part of it [13, 20].
5. Prior Notification: The case begins with the notification received by the Commission [8, 23].
Justifications: Apple and Netflix argue that the merger will lead to significant efficiencies and innovation benefits in the digital entertainment market [8]. When the EC reviews a concentration, the companies may seek to justify the transaction by arguing that the gains outweigh any potential harm [5]. However, the EC prohibits the merger if it concludes that the concentration would significantly impede effective competition [4].
Case 3: Commission v META/TikTok
Scenario: Executives from Meta and TikTok share detailed, sensitive information (algorithms, user engagement strategies, and advertising pricing models), suggesting coordination to "make more profit." The Commission initiates an investigation [23].
Competition Tool Applied: Anti-competitive behavior (Article 101 TFEU) [1, 2, 11, 26].
Analysis of Anti-competitive Behavior (Article 101 TFEU)
The general prohibition on restrictive agreements applies to agreements or concerted practices that restrict competition [2, 5]. The Commission must assess five criteria [11, 26]:
1. Between Undertakings: Met. Meta and TikTok are commercial entities, making them undertakings [11, 26].
2. Agreement/Concerted Practice: Met. The sharing of detailed information—including algorithms and advertising pricing models—between executives [23] constitutes a form of coordination or cooperation [2]. This action is likely characterized as a concerted practice, which is informal cooperation where parties substitute practical cooperation for the risks of competition, often by revealing strategies [26-28]. Sharing sensitive business data among competitors may be an infringement under competition law [2, 29].
3. Effect on Trade between Member States (MS): Met (Assumed). The effect must be significant enough to impact the pattern of trade between at least two Member States [26, 30]. Given their status as "giants" [23], this criterion applies [26].
4. Effect on Competition by Object or by Effect: Met. The discussion of pricing models and profit maximization between two giants [23] suggests an effect by object (a "hard core cartel") [26, 30], which includes the collusion of competitors [2].
5. Justification (Article 101(3) TFEU): Not Applicable. Justifications for anti-competitive behavior (where gains outweigh harm and consumers receive a fair share of the benefit) are generally difficult to apply to hard-core collusion cases [26, 31].
Conclusion: This conduct is characterized as anti-competitive behavior (acb) and constitutes a violation of Article 101 TFEU, which could result in a fine [26].
Position of the Observer
The junior data analyst indicated they were merely an observer and did not participate in the discussions [23].
• Role of Observer/Awareness: In assessing a concerted practice, the Commission must establish awareness [32]. The Court of Justice has noted that an undertaking may be presumed to have participated in concerted practices if it was aware of the anti-competitive content [32].
• Defense Argument: The undertaking (Meta or TikTok) could argue that the discussions were general or that specific individuals (like the observer) did not actively participate. However, awareness is key [32]. If an individual or the company wanted to avoid liability, they would need to prove they publicly distanced themselves from that practice [32]. The fact that the junior analyst was an observer does not automatically change the liability position of the undertaking they represent, especially if the undertaking failed to distance itself from the collusive behavior [32].
